# Keep the corpus unrecited, unreadable, and unmemorized

A self-hosted Postgres+pgvector RAG system over one author's unpublished writing faces two distinct leaks, and the research settles both with specific, buildable answers. For recitation control, no single mechanism works: the evidenced design is a four-layer gate — a system prompt (which alone lifts CoTaEval's regurgitation-reduction win rate from **25.4% to 59.1%** at near-zero cost but "does not correctly reject every instance" ([CoTaEval, arXiv:2406.18664v4](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.18664v4))), a normalized n-gram + LCS detector against the retrieved chunks, a BloomScrub-style rewrite-or-abstain loop, and an owner bypass so the author can read their own text. For confidentiality under compromise, the dominant finding is that the **pgvector column is a near-complete copy of the corpus**: vec2text recovers **92% of 32-token inputs exactly** from dense embeddings ([Morris et al., arXiv:2310.06816](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.06816)), and because bge-m3 is open-weights the attacker is in the strongest possible position — exact encoder in hand, unlimited offline queries. Encrypting the chunk text with Cloak while leaving vectors and any tsvector index in plaintext is "redacting the document but shipping its photocopy." The clean escape at this corpus's scale (10³–10⁵ chunks) is to store vectors AES-GCM-encrypted and run exact brute-force kNN in application memory, removing plaintext vectors from the database, its backups, and its branches entirely. And a later LoRA is the sharpest knife of all: a canary repeated just **16 times reproduced in full** on a LoRA fine-tune of LLaMA-2 7B ([Savine, arXiv:2507.21009](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.21009v2)) — a threshold a hundred-document corpus trained for a few epochs hits for *every* document — so the right sequence is RAG now, LoRA only behind a pre-ship extraction eval. The recommended hobby-scale posture, strongest-first, is below.

## A private corpus needs a four-layer recitation gate, because a system prompt leaks on retry

The policy "the corpus may teach but never recite" maps onto a mature detection stack, but every layer has a documented failure mode that forces the next. The canonical definition of "verbatim" is **a substring of at least 50 tokens** present in the source, scored by binary search over a suffix array and filtered to high-entropy spans so boilerplate doesn't trigger; the value was chosen by doubling chance news-article overlap, and re-running at 40 and 100 tokens preserved model rankings ([Nasr/Carlini et al., arXiv:2311.17035](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2311.17035)). That same 50-token threshold is the de-facto standard in training-data deduplication ([Lee et al., arXiv:2107.06499](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2107.06499)). Below the unambiguous-verbatim line sits a gray band — roughly **8–50 normalized tokens** — where distinctiveness, not length, should decide, and below ~**3–4 words** everything is noise by CoTaEval's own ACS floor ([CoTaEval, arXiv:2406.18664v4](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.18664v4)).

A system prompt is the cheapest and, in RAG, often the single most effective intervention, but it **fails unpredictably per instance and loses force exactly when the text sits in context** — a 43,200-query benchmark found models refuse direct requests for copyrighted text yet comply when the same text is placed in retrieved context, even with an explicit copyright notice present ([arXiv:2411.01136](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2411.01136v1)). This is the crux for RAG: the retrieved chunk is already in the prompt, which is why the detector must run **against the retrieved chunks specifically**, not only the whole corpus. Exact-match output filters (MemFree's decoding-time guard, which rejects any token completing a training n-gram and "guarantees zero verbatim n-gram reproduction") are "necessary but not sufficient" — they are bypassed by minor misspellings, extra whitespace, or newline changes, and "the model tends to make these edits" spontaneously ([MemFree/Ippolito et al., arXiv:2210.17546](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2210.17546)). The fix is normalization plus a near-duplicate metric: CoTaEval's recipe lowercases and strips whitespace and punctuation before computing LCS, word-level ACS (cumulative matching subsequences longer than 3 words, which catches "stitched" quoting), ROUGE-L recall, and MinHash Jaccard at ~0.8 ([CoTaEval, arXiv:2406.18664v4](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.18664v4); [DataComp-LM, arXiv:2406.11794](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.11794)). On detection, the published post-hoc architecture is **BloomScrub: detect the span, feed it to a rewrite model, re-check, and abstain if a long quote survives** — yielding a certified guarantee of no long verbatim quotes ([BloomScrub, arXiv:2504.16046](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.16046v2)).

Deployed systems validate both the shape and the pitfalls. GitHub Copilot's duplication filter compares a ~150-character whitespace-stripped lexeme window against indexed public code and either blocks or, via "code referencing," **discloses the match, source, and license instead of suppressing** ([GitHub Docs](https://docs.github.com/en/copilot/how-tos/get-code-suggestions/find-matching-code?tool=webui); [GitHub blog](https://github.blog/engineering/introducing-code-referencing-for-github-copilot/)). Google Gemini's non-configurable `RECITATION` finish-reason kills the response and **repeatedly false-positives on the user's own content** — translating one's own website, processing one's own legal documents — sometimes returning an empty candidate that crashes strict SDK parsers ([Gemini troubleshooting](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/troubleshooting.md.txt); [Google AI forum](https://discuss.ai.google.dev/t/finishreason-recitation-issue-wtih-my-own-content-via-api/69104)). That failure is the decisive design lesson for a *single-author* corpus: when the corpus owner is the user, a blanket recitation filter fights legitimate use, so the gate must distinguish *whose* text is reproduced and *who is asking*, with an **owner/role bypass** — something provider filters structurally cannot do. The threat model must also include cooperative-adversarial retrying: the Dow Jones/New York Post v. Perplexity complaint alleges verbatim article reproduction coaxed by users who "were pleading with Perplexity to repeat their articles verbatim," and CopyBreakRAG extracted **over 70% of a target application's knowledge base verbatim** via adversarial queries ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2024/10/21/content-kleptocracy-news-corp-outlets-sue-perplexity-over-scraped-stories); [arXiv:2411.14110v2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2411.14110v2)). A stateless filter that fails 1-in-N is defeated by a retry loop.

The task's second half — "still surfacing its key arguments and bibliographic references" — is served by the same gray-zone machinery run *permissively*. Low-distinctiveness spans are deliberately exempted in both deployed and research systems: Copilot passes snippets "too generic to flag," Nasr et al. require high entropy before counting a match, and ACS ignores matches under 3 words ([CoTaEval](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.18664v4)). Titles, author names, and bibliographic strings are low-entropy/high-frequency and pass a distinctiveness (corpus-IDF/entropy) test; for a small single-author corpus where even a citation string may be rare, a **structural allowlist for emitted citation patterns** (title + author + date) is safer than frequency alone. Paraphrased *arguments* — "non-literal copying" in CopyBench's terms, which rose from 2.3% to 5.9% from Llama3-8B to 70B — are "not reliably stoppable by any current inference-time method," so the right treatment is to **score and log semantic similarity for review, not block** ([CopyBench, arXiv:2407.07087](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.07087v1)). Legally there is no bright line to lean on: no statutory word count exists, and in *Harper & Row* quoting ~300 words from a ~30,000-word unpublished manuscript was held *not* fair use because it was the heart of the work ([FindLaw](https://corporate.findlaw.com/intellectual-property/fair-use-interpretation-amp-guidelines-the-fair-use-doctrine.html)) — doubly relevant here, since for a *private, unpublished* corpus disclosure itself is the harm, so the owner's policy, not fair use, governs the quote budget (tens of words, attributed, far below the 200–400-word publisher ask-permission band).

## The embedding column is a near-complete copy of the corpus, and bge-m3 is the worst case

The foundational result is that embeddings "reveal (almost) as much as text." Vec2Text frames inversion as iterative correct-and-re-embed and reaches **BLEU 97.3, token F1 99, and 92% exact reconstruction on 32-token GTR inputs**; on clinical notes it recovered **94.2% of first names, 95.3% of last names, and 89.2% of full names** even where only 26% of notes were exact — entity leakage far exceeds verbatim leakage ([arXiv:2310.06816](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.06816)). Fidelity falls with length — longer inputs (~128 tokens, trec-news) drop to **BLEU 14.5 / token F1 51.5** — so prose chunks of 200–500 tokens should be presumed to leak *high-fidelity gist, named entities, distinctive vocabulary, and plot/topic structure* per chunk rather than verbatim text ([ar5iv 2310.06816](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2310.06816)). For unpublished creative writing, gist plus character names plus plot structure, across every chunk in order, is close to total confidentiality loss even without a single verbatim sentence.

What makes this deployment worst-case is that **bge-m3 is open-weights**. Every inversion attack's binding constraint is query access to the encoder; an open model hands the attacker the exact encoder, unlimited free offline queries, the ability to train a dedicated inverter at leisure, and unlimited (text, embedding) pairs. The "attacker doesn't know it's bge-m3" defense is empty — 1024 dimensions narrows the field to a handful of popular models, and probe-embedding against each fingerprints the encoder in minutes. The 2025–2026 attack generation removed the old requirements entirely: **ALGEN** uses a closed-form least-squares map to align victim embeddings into the attacker's own space, so **~30 leaked pairs reach ROUGE-L > 20 and 1,000 pairs reach ROUGE-L ~41–46 at cosine ~0.93** across T5, GTR, mBERT, and OpenAI models ([ALGEN, arXiv:2502.11308](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.11308v2)); **ZSinvert** is zero-shot and stays effective under Gaussian noise up to σ=0.01 ([arXiv:2504.00147](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.00147v1)); a 2026 masked-diffusion method recovers **up to 81.3% of tokens from a single embedding** ([arXiv:2602.11047](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2602.11047)). BGE-family models are confirmed targets, attack accuracy *rises* with more expressive encoders, and multilingual models (bge-m3's class) may be *more* vulnerable, with English-only defenses failing to carry over ([arXiv:2404.16587](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2404.16587v1.pdf); [arXiv:2401.12192](https://arxiv.org/html/2401.12192v3)). Even short of full inversion, an attacker holding a suspected excerpt can embed it and check for a near-1.0 cosine neighbor — a **verbatim-passage-confirmation** attack needing no decoder, no training, and one embedding call, which is itself a breach for pre-publication work (leak attribution, scooping).

The costly truth about mitigations is that **cheap geometry obfuscation fails against a known-model attacker**. Against the original vec2text, Gaussian noise (λ=0.01: BLEU 80.4→10.3 at ~2% retrieval cost), dimension reduction (768→256 cut exact-match 43.0→5.9 at top-10 0.748→0.731), and a secret per-user scaling transform (BLEU 1.1 / exact 0.0 at identical retrieval) all looked excellent ([arXiv:2402.12784](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.12784)). But ALGEN then showed the generic break: its least-squares alignment defeats *any* unknown linear view of the space, so linear-transform watermarking and dimension shuffling reduced ROUGE-L only marginally (42.9→39.4 and →35.3), and the authors conclude "none of the established defense mechanisms" protects embeddings while preserving utility ([arXiv:2502.11308](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.11308v2)). Noise is further beaten by ZSinvert and BeamClean, and under ALGEN, noise large enough to cut ROUGE-L to ~13 also collapses downstream accuracy ([arXiv:2505.13758](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.13758v1)). The one cheap defense still standing is **product/binary quantization** — it reduced vec2text exact-match to **0.0 with retrieval essentially unchanged (top-10 0.749 vs 0.748)**, and ALGEN explicitly left it untested ([arXiv:2402.12784](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.12784)) — but absence of a published attack is not proof of resistance, and an author's own *published* work is a ready supply of the known plaintexts an alignment attack feeds on.

This is why the corpus's small scale is the real lever. All the "vectors must be plaintext for HNSW" pressure assumes the ANN index lives in the database. A single author's corpus is ~10³–10⁵ chunks, and exact brute-force kNN over that many 1024-dim vectors in application memory is milliseconds. **The app can store vectors Cloak-encrypted (AES-GCM) in Postgres, decrypt on boot into an in-memory index, and never give the database plaintext vectors at all** — eliminating the DB-exfiltration inversion threat entirely at the cost of startup load time and app-memory residency. This is the only architecture where at-rest encryption and semantic search coexist without vendor products or research crypto, because no production-ready encrypted-ANN scheme exists ("searching over ciphertext in vector stores remains an open research area," [arXiv:2603.21654](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.21654)). The one caveat to verify before relying on DELETE for erasure: "Ghost Vectors" studies soft-deleted embeddings remaining reconstructible in HNSW indexes, and pgvector dead-marks rather than rebuilds until vacuum/reindex ([arXiv:2606.18497](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.18497)) — so in-app indexing sidesteps this too, since there is no on-disk ANN graph to leave ghosts in.

## Encrypting text while vectors and the tsvector index stay plaintext ships the photocopy

The regulatory and vendor consensus now treats embeddings as sensitive: **OWASP made "Vector and Embedding Weaknesses" a Top-10 LLM risk in 2025** with embedding inversion named explicitly, and EnterpriseDB's pgvector docs instruct "if the source content is sensitive, treat embeddings as sensitive data" ([OWASP LLM08:2025](https://genai.owasp.org/llmrisk/llm082025-vector-and-embedding-weaknesses/); [EDB](https://www.enterprisedb.com/docs/pg_extensions/pgvector/security/)). On the legal side the EDPB's Opinion 28/2024 rejects blanket anonymity for AI artifacts derived from personal data, setting the bar at *negligible* extraction likelihood — a test current inversion fidelity clearly fails ([EDPB Opinion 28/2024](https://edpb.europa.eu/system/files/2024-12/edpb_opinion_202428_ai-models_en.pdf)). The defensible position: embeddings of the author's unpublished writing carry the source text's full confidentiality classification, so they must be reachable by erasure, included in export, and kept out of event_log/audit/warehouse exactly like text PII.

The full-text-search index is the quieter copy, and it is nearly a plaintext one. A **tsvector stores stemmed lexemes with integer position numbers**, and extraction is a built-in function: `unnest(tsvector)` expands it to lexeme/position rows and `tsvector_to_array` returns the lexeme array ([PostgreSQL docs](https://www.postgresql.org/docs/current/textsearch-features.html)). Ordering those lexemes by position yields a stemmed, stop-word-stripped transcript — "treat tsvector over the corpus as plaintext corpus," and a GIN index persists those lexemes into base backups and WAL. A plaintext `pg_trgm` index is worse (raw character trigrams, no stemming loss). The only acceptable keyword-search variant for sensitive prose is a **blinded index**: SysReptor's production design normalizes text, splits into UTF-8 trigrams, HMAC-SHA256-keys each with the app encryption key, and stores only 16-byte tokens — but it acknowledges frequency-analysis leakage (which documents share content) and dictionary probes by anyone holding the key ([SysReptor](https://docs.sysreptor.com/insights/project-search)). CipherStash's EQL offers Bloom-filtered n-gram "match" indexing but states it is "unsuitable for paragraph-scale text, such as searching a full novel" ([CipherStash](https://cipherstash.com/llms/md/blog/searchable-encryption-in-postgres)). The decision gradient is therefore clear: **vector-only retrieval (no tsvector/trgm on corpus text) is the zero-leakage, zero-new-machinery default**; an HMAC-blinded trigram table is the proven DIY upgrade only if keyword search is later demanded; EQL is overkill at hobby scale and weak for prose anyway.

Application-layer AEAD is what draws the only boundary the provider cannot cross. Cloak's default cipher is **AES.GCM with a 256-bit key read from an environment variable** (`decode_env!("CLOAK_KEY")`), ciphertexts self-tagged with key identity, and rotation handled by coexisting keys + lazy re-encrypt on read + `mix cloak.migrate.ecto` to retire the old key ([cloak_ecto](https://cloak-ecto.hexdocs.pm/install.html); [key rotation](https://hexdocs.pm/cloak_ecto/rotate_keys.html)). Its guarantee, assembled from the Rails analogue: **protects** against Neon console/DB-viewer reads, pg_dump theft, backup/branch/snapshot theft, SQL-injection `SELECT` (attacker gets ciphertext binaries), any role with table SELECT, and provider-side storage compromise — because only ciphertext reaches Postgres. It **does not protect** against compromise of the app VM (key in env, plaintext in BEAM memory), exfiltration of `CLOAK_KEY`, or anything the app legitimately decrypts and then logs, caches, or sends onward ([Rails AR Encryption](https://guides.rubyonrails.org/active_record_encryption.html); [CipherStash](https://cipherstash.com/docs/solutions/ai-and-rag)). The three database layers answer three different attackers and must not be confused: **role GRANTs stop other DB principals, RLS stops the app's own bugs crossing row boundaries, and app-layer encryption stops everyone who sees the bytes without the key** — but RLS and roles contribute *nothing* against a provider-side or storage-level read, since they are enforced by the very Postgres process the provider operates. Neon encrypts storage with AES-256 under *its own* AWS KMS/Azure Key Vault keys, decrypted transparently on every page read, so any credentialed or provider-side read sees plaintext columns, every pgvector embedding, every tsvector/GIN index, all WAL, and all branches ([Neon security](https://neon.com/docs/security/security-overview)); Neon routes column confidentiality to pgcrypto or app-side encryption and leaves RLS to the customer ([Neon HIPAA](https://neon.com/guides/hipaa-compliant-applications)). A critical project-specific trap: **this repo's local tests connect as superuser, bypassing RLS and GRANTs**, so any new policy on vector/chunk tables is untested unless probed with `SET LOCAL ROLE stacks_app`.

Two operational facts close the at-rest story. First, the latency objection to encrypting chunk text is spurious: AES-GCM on hardware runs at gigabytes/second, so decrypting top-k (~5–20 chunks of 1–2 KB) is microseconds — orders of magnitude below the embedding and LLM calls in the same request ([CipherStash](https://cipherstash.com/docs/solutions/ai-and-rag)). The real hazard is downstream leakage: **decrypted chunks escaping into app logs, LLM-observability traces, tool spans, and error reports, which regex redaction provably does not fully catch** — Grafana's sanitizer skips user messages by default and "sensitive content in a model's output reaches traces unmodified" ([Grafana](https://grafana.com/docs/grafana-cloud/machine-learning/ai-observability/privacy-and-security/pii-and-secrets-redaction/); [OneUptime](https://oneuptime.com/blog/post/2026-11-16-how-to-redact-sensitive-llm-data-across-prompt-logs-tool-spans-and-error-reports/view)). The assembled prompt contains decrypted corpus text, and any ETS/Redis cache of retrieved chunks recreates a plaintext store outside the encryption boundary and outside erasure's reach. Second, **every byte Postgres stores travels intact into dumps, WAL, replicas, and Neon copy-on-write branches** — so preview/dev branches duplicate the corpus exposure surface and must either exclude the corpus table or carry an empty one. The upside: ciphertext stays ciphertext everywhere, and if the whole corpus is erased, **destroying `CLOAK_KEY` crypto-shreds every stray backup for free**.

## A style LoRA memorizes a hundred-document corpus at sixteen repetitions

If the corpus is ever used to fine-tune, the risk class changes from "what retrieval surfaces" to "what the weights baked in," and small-corpus economics make it acute. Memorization grows log-linearly with model capacity, example duplication, and context length ([Carlini et al., arXiv:2202.07646](https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.07646)), and in fine-tuning it grows with epochs and rank. The dominant number: on a **LoRA fine-tune of LLaMA-2 7B over ~3,000 examples — exactly small-corpus scale — a canary repeated 16× was reproduced in full at temperature 0.1, and at 32× it was reproduced in full at every temperature tested** ([Savine, arXiv:2507.21009](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.21009v2)). A corpus of dozens-to-hundreds of documents trained for a typical 3–10 epochs puts *every* document in that 16–32 effective-repetition regime, whereas a typical pretraining example is seen once and is unlikely to be extractable — so per-example verbatim extraction is *materially more likely* for a small fine-tune. Worse, drafts and revisions of one manuscript are **fuzzy duplicates**, and near-duplicates contribute to memorization almost as much as exact copies, so exact-match dedup undercounts ([Mosaic Memory, arXiv:2405.15523](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.15523v2)). A writing assistant is the worst-case *task* type too: open-ended generation in the author's voice is the most memorization-prone regime, and the stylistic goal actively rewards reproducing the corpus's phrasing ([Zeng et al., arXiv:2310.06714](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.06714v2)).

The attack surface is black-box — anyone who can chat with the assistant qualifies. Prefix→suffix extraction fully recovered 32×-repeated sequences at all temperatures; the divergence attack lifted ChatGPT's training-data emission **150× and recovered >10,000 verbatim examples for ~$200**, proving "alignment does not remove memorization" ([arXiv:2311.17035](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.17035)). Most striking for unpublished work, **membership inference against LoRA fine-tunes reaches AUC ≈0.924** (SPV-MIA, with LoRA as the default PEFT method), near a reliable oracle for "was this passage in the private corpus" ([Fu et al., arXiv:2311.06062](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.06062)). For unpublished writing, that confirmation is itself a breach — the leak threshold is *below* verbatim extraction. And the adapter file is a sensitive artifact in its own right: in the nearest studied setting (diffusion LoRAs), an adversary holding only the shared weights trained an encoder that **reconstructed the private training images**, and DP defenses did not fully prevent it without hurting utility ([arXiv:2409.08482](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2409.08482)). The adapter, its checkpoints, optimizer states, and eval logs (which quote the corpus) are all shadow copies of the corpus and inherit its classification and its erasure obligation — and since no reliable per-document "unlearning" is established, erasure means retrain-or-delete the adapter.

The mitigations, by evidence strength, are **near-duplicate deduplication (~10× reduction), minimal epochs/early stopping, and a low-rank + dropout + attention-placement LoRA config**, with DP-SGD as the only formal guarantee. The config matters measurably: at rank 16 with dropout, LoRA stayed near-zero on plagiarism metrics while full fine-tuning exceeded 50% paraphrase-plagiarism at duplication ρ=20; raising rank/α from 4 to 32 nudged max similarity only 0.70→0.75; and MIA AUC rises with rank, with value/output matrices leaking more than query/key ([Wang & Li, arXiv:2506.20856](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.20856); [Savine, arXiv:2507.21009](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.21009v2)). The two headline LoRA studies disagree — Wang & Li find LoRA near-zero and flat in duplication, Savine finds a LoRA fully reproducing 16× canaries — so "LoRA is low-risk" is **configuration-dependent, not a guarantee**. DP-SGD gives the best privacy-utility trade-off but is expensive and costs exactly the stylistic fidelity the assistant exists for; usefully, the literature finds **LoRA alone lands "almost on par with DP" on empirical privacy at far lower cost** ([arXiv:2502.13313](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.13313v2)), so DP is reserved for when the corpus contains third parties' personal data. The pre-ship gate is an **extraction eval**: plant high-entropy canaries at 1×/4×/16×, then measure canary exposure, run prefix→suffix extraction over real documents at several temperatures, and run an SPV-MIA with held-out author text as non-members — shipping only if real-document suffix recovery is near zero. Single metrics underestimate exposure, and weak canaries explain why frontier-lab studies report little memorization while extraction research recovers data from the same models, so the audit must combine perplexity, extraction probes, and MIA ([ICLR 2025, arXiv:2503.06808](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.06808v1)).

The architectural conclusion is that **RAG-only eliminates the entire weight-memorization class** — nothing about the corpus enters a trainable artifact, erasure is `delete row + embedding` rather than a retrain, and per-document access control is enforceable at the retriever. It is also categorically easier for GDPR right-to-erasure. RAG substitutes a different, revocable channel: prompt-level extraction has reached **up to 84.4% coverage of a RAG corpus** within a fixed query budget and RAG-Thief automates datastore extraction at ~3× prior rates ([arXiv:2601.15678](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.15678v1); [RAG-Thief, arXiv:2411.14110](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.14110v1)), but for a single-user private assistant this collapses to an access-control problem — who can query, and who can read the vector store — rather than a model-behavior problem. The engineering ordering (prompt → few-shot → RAG → fine-tune, where fine-tuning buys *form* and RAG buys *facts*) coincides with the privacy ordering: the thing a style-LoRA buys is precisely what memorization-prone generation leaks ([AI Engineering, ch. 7](https://oreilly.com/library/view/ai-engineering/9781098166298/ch07.html)).

## The recommended posture: brute-force kNN, encrypt everything, defer the LoRA

For a hobby-scale deployment the layered posture, strongest-first, is concrete. On **confidentiality**: (1) don't embed what needn't be embedded, and strip direct identifiers pre-embedding where feasible; (2) store vectors Cloak-encrypted and run exact kNN in application memory, keeping plaintext vectors out of the DB, backups, and branches entirely — the decisive move this corpus's scale makes free; (3) if vectors must stay in-DB for any reason, apply aggressive quantization as the best currently-unbroken cheap hardening *and* document inversion as a residual risk equal to source-text disclosure; (4) treat noise/rotation/shuffling as non-defenses given an open encoder; (5) encrypt chunk text and sensitive metadata with app-layer AES-GCM, decrypt only top-k at prompt-assembly; (6) default to **vector-only retrieval with no tsvector/trgm index** over corpus text; (7) run the app as a dedicated least-privilege role with RLS, probed under `SET LOCAL ROLE` because superuser tests hide the layer; (8) keep decrypted chunks out of logs, traces, caches, event_log, audit, and warehouse; (9) exclude the corpus from preview branches; (10) make erasure delete rows and destroy/rotate `CLOAK_KEY` for crypto-shredding. On **recitation**: a system-prompt rule, a normalized n-gram + LCS/ACS detector against retrieved chunks, a rewrite-or-abstain loop, a distinctiveness/allowlist path that lets bibliographic strings and paraphrased arguments through while blocking distinctive prose, an owner bypass, and a CoTaEval-style regression eval. On **LoRA**: stay RAG-only; if ever fine-tuning, use near-dup dedup + few epochs + low rank + dropout + attention placement, treat the adapter at the corpus's classification, and gate the ship on a canary/extraction/MIA eval.

| Layer | Attacker it stops | Attacker it does *not* stop | Evidence status |
|---|---|---|---|
| Role GRANTs + RLS | Other DB principals; app-logic row-crossing bugs | Provider/storage read; dump/backup/branch; superuser | Proven mechanism; **untested locally** (superuser bypass) |
| Disk/volume + Neon at-rest AES-256 | Stolen media | Any credentialed or provider-side read | Proven; provider holds the keys |
| App-layer AES-GCM on chunk text | DB bytes everywhere (dump, backup, branch, injection, provider) | App-VM compromise; `CLOAK_KEY` exfil; post-decrypt logging | Proven for its scope |
| Vectors in-DB (plaintext) | — | Inversion → gist+entities (near-verbatim ≤32 tok) | **Broken**: 92% exact @32 tok; ALGEN breaks obfuscation |
| Quantized vectors in-DB | vec2text (exact→0.0) | Possibly alignment attacks (untested) | Cheap; **adversarially under-tested** |
| Encrypted vectors + in-app kNN | DB-exfiltration inversion entirely | App-VM compromise | Sound by construction; **no published kNN-vs-HNSW benchmark at this scale** |
| tsvector/GIN over corpus | — | Anyone reading the index (≈ plaintext) | **Broken**: built-in extraction |
| Recitation gate (4-layer) | Casual + most adversarial verbatim | Non-literal/paraphrase reproduction | Proven partial; no method excels on all metrics |
| RAG-only (vs LoRA) | Weight memorization entirely | Prompt-extraction via query access | Proven class-elimination |
| LoRA + dedup/low-rank/dropout/eval | Most verbatim at good config | Config-dependent; MIA AUC ~0.9 persists | **Mixed**: two studies disagree |

## Conclusion

The two questions resolve to one architectural decision and one discipline. The decision is to **keep the corpus out of every artifact the database or a future adapter could hand an attacker** — vectors encrypted and searched in app memory, text encrypted at rest, no full-text index, and no LoRA until an extraction eval proves the adapter doesn't recite. At 10³–10⁵ chunks this is not a compromise between security and retrievability; brute-force kNN makes encryption-at-rest and semantic search fully compatible, which is exactly the trade every vendor scheme is still trying to buy at hobby scale and failing to. The discipline is to stop pretending any derivative of the corpus is inert: the embedding is the corpus minus verbatim fidelity at long chunk lengths and minus nothing at short ones, the tsvector is the corpus transcribed, and the adapter is the corpus lossily compressed with a 0.9-AUC membership oracle attached.

What is genuinely proven: embedding inversion at short lengths, the failure of geometry-obfuscation defenses against an open encoder, tsvector extractability, the microsecond cost of top-k decryption, the 16–32-repetition memorization threshold, and RAG's elimination of weight memorization. What remains uncertain and should be flagged in any design doc: no attack has measured 256–1000-token chunks (gist-level leakage is extrapolation), quantization's resistance to alignment attacks is untested rather than disproven, the two LoRA studies disagree on how safe a well-configured adapter is, no regulator has ruled on vector stores specifically, and there is no published benchmark for in-app kNN at this scale or for multi-turn retry-until-leak recitation dynamics. The honest posture is to build for the proven threats, treat the uncertain ones as live until measured, and — because the corpus *is* the person under GDPR — make every copy, including backups, branches, caches, adapters, and eval logs, reachable by the same erasure path that already governs the author's text.
