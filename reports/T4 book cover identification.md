# Cut the model, not the GPU

**The T4 is the wrong lever.** On the best arithmetic available it saves roughly 10% per identification against the measured A10G baseline, and on the only published Turing VLM throughput measurement it is both slower *and* dearer — break-even against 9.7s on an A10G sits at **18.1 seconds**, and the one measured figure is 5–20 tokens/s on a Tesla T4 using *two* of them ([vLLM issue #29743](https://github.com/vllm-project/vllm/issues/29743)). The lever that actually pays is the model. Qwen2.5-VL-7B in fp16 is **~16 GB of weights against 16 GB of VRAM** — it does not fit a T4 at all, so any T4 plan is already a plan to run something smaller; and the smaller thing worth running, **Qwen3-VL-4B under Apache-2.0**, scores 63.70% on OCRBench-v2 English where the 7B baseline scores 41.8% ([llmboard](https://www.llmboard.ai/benchmarks/ocrbench-v2-en-456); [OCRBench v2](https://99franklin.github.io/ocrbench_v2/)), halves the weight bytes that dominate the 20.5s cold load, and runs on the A10G you already have. Specialising a lighter model before deployment is viable — but only for *selection behaviour*, not for cover identity, because **no book-cover dataset anywhere carries an affirmative licence on its pixels**, and because the dominant retrieval failure is index scale, not photograph quality: visual top-1 collapses 0.68 → 0.18 against 104k distractors while OCR degrades only 0.65 → 0.31 ([Shih et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1411.5307)). The JEPA family has no role: I-JEPA and V-JEPA 1 are CC-BY-NC, the smallest checkpoint is 600M, and no JEPA model has ever been evaluated on a retrieval benchmark.

## Sixteen gigabytes eliminates the measured baseline before any benchmark runs

The T4 is 16 GB of GDDR6 at **320 GB/s**, 65 FP16 TFLOPS, 130 INT8 TOPS, 260 INT4 TOPS, 70 W, compute capability 7.5 ([NVIDIA](https://www.nvidia.com/en-us/data-center/tesla-t4/)). The A10G it would replace runs at **600 GB/s** ([Flopper](https://flopper.io/gpu/nvidia-a10g-24gb)) — a 0.53× bandwidth ratio, and since autoregressive decode re-reads the whole weight set per token, that ratio is the floor on decode speed before any kernel penalty. The compute ratio is near parity on paper (65 vs 70 FP16 TFLOPS), though the A10G figure is contested: other listings give 125 TFLOPS for the A10, and NVIDIA publishes no A10G-specific datasheet, so the hardware note carries the range rather than resolving it.

The binding constraint is not bandwidth, though. Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct is listed as **"8B params"** ([model card](https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct)), which is ~16.0 GB in fp16 — equal to the entire card before KV cache, activations, or CUDA context. The arithmetic admits only three shapes:

| Configuration | Weights (derived, fp16 = 2 B/param) | Fits 16 GB? |
|---|---|---|
| 7B-class fp16 | ~16.0 GB | **No** |
| 7B-class INT8 W8A8 | ~8.0 GB | Yes |
| 7B-class 4-bit, ViT left in fp16 | ~5.9 GB | Yes |
| 4B-class fp16 | ~8 GB | Yes |
| 3B-class fp16 | ~6–7.6 GB | Yes |

KV cache is not the problem — 56 KiB/token for the 7B, 1.75 GiB at 32k context. **Vision tokens are.** An unresized 12 MP phone photo produces **15,552 LLM visual tokens and 62,208 ViT patches** at Qwen2.5-VL's default limits (derived from `patch_size 14`, `spatial_merge_size 2` in the [config](https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct/raw/main/config.json)); a single activation tensor in the vision tower is then 159 MiB across 32 layers, and any attention kernel materialising a 62,208² logits matrix is arithmetically impossible. SmolVLM's measured **4.9 GB for a 2.2B model versus 13.7 GB for Qwen2-VL-2B** at the same parameter count ([arXiv 2504.05299](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2504.05299)) is the cleanest proof that visual-token policy, not parameter count, decides whether a VLM fits.

Turing's architectural gaps compound this. There is **no bfloat16** ([turing-bf16](https://github.com/tech-cosmos/turing-bf16)), and Qwen2.5-VL is bf16 by design — its config declares `torch_dtype: bfloat16` and the official vLLM recipe gives no fp16 guidance at all ([vLLM recipe](https://docs.vllm.ai/projects/recipes/en/stable/Qwen/Qwen2.5-VL.html)). The fp16 failure is documented and reproduced: Qwen2.5-VL-**3B** emitted a stream of `!!!` on certain images under `--dtype half`, on a Turing 2080 Ti and an L40S alike, while **"72b & 7b were okay"** ([vLLM #13817](https://github.com/vllm-project/vllm/issues/13817)). The size that fits a T4 is the size with the defect, and it fails *silently* — a confident wrong answer, not an exception. There is no FlashAttention-2 (SM80+ only), no FP8, and **no Marlin**, which vLLM's own matrix confirms is Ampere-and-up ([vLLM quantization hardware](https://docs.vllm.ai/en/v0.9.0/features/quantization/supported_hardware.html)). So 4-bit on a T4 is compression without speed: it loads, it saves memory, it falls back to the slow dequant-then-fp16 kernels, and Turing's own 260 INT4 TOPS is unreachable through any mainstream serving stack. TensorRT-LLM has dropped Turing from its supported-hardware page entirely ([TensorRT-LLM](https://nvidia.github.io/TensorRT-LLM/supported-hardware.html)); SGLang has removed the non-Marlin GPTQ path on NVIDIA ([SGLang](https://docs.sglang.ai/advanced_features/quantization.html)). PyTorch keeps sm_75 through CUDA 13.4 ([pytorch #190385](https://github.com/pytorch/pytorch/issues/190385)), so the floor holds — but the per-model VLM backend whitelists are where Turing dies, and the issue tracking it was closed "not planned" after going stale.

**Two gaps must travel with these numbers.** Nobody has published a measured VRAM figure for Qwen2.5-VL at any size on a T4 — every fit number above is arithmetic. And issue #29743 concerns **Qwen3-VL**, not Qwen2.5-VL; whether the older model's vLLM implementation carries the same backend whitelist is unverified, and it is the single cheapest thing to check before trusting or discarding the T4.

## What makes a T4 thinkable is the architecture you already have

Three things would make accuracy and speed survivable on 16 GB, and they are closer to one thing than three. **A resolution cap is the master lever.** Capping `max_pixels` at 1,003,520 (1,280 visual tokens) was the maintainer's workaround for the fp16 garbage output, is what makes the ViT forward pass fit, and cuts prefill — the same cap buys numerical safety, memory headroom, and speed simultaneously. A book cover is a single large title at high contrast and low information density, so the cap is cheap; whether 1,280 tokens suffices is an assumption to measure against the project's own corpus, not a settled fact.

**The ISBN hard gate is the backstop that makes the silent-failure mode tolerable.** The dangerous shape of fp16 overflow on Turing is a fluent wrong title, not a crash — and the project's existing requirement that every book resolve against Open Library or Google Books is exactly the validator that catches it. A T4 deployment without that gate would be reckless; with it, the risk is a retry, not a corrupt record.

**The cascade decides whether the VLM tier is hit often enough to matter at all.** The project's barcode → cover-embedding → OCR → VLM ordering is validated rather than overturned by this research: the live question is what fraction each tier absorbs, because a VLM tier handling 15% of uploads can afford to be slower, or can stay on a larger model, inside $50/month. Measuring that split is worth more than any further model comparison.

Within those constraints, **INT8 W8A8 is the quietly reasonable T4 configuration** — supported on Turing, native 130 INT8 TOPS at 2× the fp16 rate, halves a 7B-class model to ~8 GB, and unlike AWQ does not force an fp16-only checkpoint (AWQ checkpoints "only support half (float16) dtype", and the thread reports the overflow problem is *worse* there). No T4 W8A8 VLM benchmark exists anywhere in this research, so this is a hypothesis to measure, not a finding.

The economics, though, keep pointing away. At $1.10/hr the A10G baseline costs **$0.00296 per identification** (9.7s), so $50/month buys roughly 16,900 warm identifications. The T4 at $0.59/hr is 1.86× cheaper per hour, putting break-even at **18.1 seconds**. A naive bandwidth-scaled estimate lands at ~16.3s — about 10% cheaper for 1.7× worse latency, i.e. **$5/month of saving**. The measured 5–20 tok/s figure puts it past break-even entirely. Meanwhile cold start, which at low traffic dominates everything, is not a PCIe problem: 16 GB in 20.5s is an effective **~0.8 GB/s**, roughly 1/20th of even Gen3's practical rate, so the time goes to fetching and deserialising weights. **Shrinking the weights is what pays, on any GPU.** Modal's own GPU memory snapshotting cuts a 3B cold start from ~118s to ~12s ([Modal](https://modal.com/blog/mistral-3)), but it is explicitly experimental, it is documented against vLLM sleep mode — the exact stack whose Turing VLM support is broken-by-default — and no source states whether it supports Turing at all.

## A newer 4B model beats the baseline before anyone specialises anything

The small-models research produces the finding that reorders the whole decision: **Qwen3-VL-4B-Instruct is Apache-2.0 on its official card** ([model card](https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen3-VL-4B-Instruct)) and ranks 8th on OCRBench-v2 English at **63.70%**, against Qwen2.5-VL-7B's **41.8%** on the same v2 scale. If that transfers, the small-versus-large trade-off the project assumed has been dissolved by a generation change rather than by any clever engineering — a 4B 2026 model plausibly beats the measured 7B 2025 baseline *before* specialisation, while halving the weight bytes that dominate cold load (a derived ~10s instead of 20.5s at the observed 0.8 GB/s) and cutting decode cost on the hardware already in place.

That claim needs its caveats carried intact. Qwen3-VL-4B's own card publishes **no numeric OCR scores** — its performance charts are images — so 63.70% is a leaderboard row, not an author-published figure, and a third-party guide gives conflicting numbers around 85–86%. There are at least three incompatible "OCRBench" scales in circulation (raw out of 1000, the same divided by 10, and OCRBench v2 as a percentage), and a 822 and a 47.5% in the same table are not rankable. Most importantly, **no book-cover benchmark exists in any of these leaderboards** — every published score is on documents, charts and scene text, none on a photograph of a cover with glare, skew and stylised display type. The project's own 91% measurement is more informative than any of them.

The licence filter prunes the field hard, and one trap is worth stating because it is invisible. **Qwen2.5-VL-3B — the obvious "just shrink it" move — ships under the Qwen Research License, non-commercial only**, while its 7B and 72B siblings are Apache-2.0 ([LICENSE](https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen2.5-VL-3B-Instruct/blob/main/LICENSE)); Jina AI had to contact the Qwen team directly for permission when building on it. PaliGemma 2 fails on precisely the DINOv3 grounds: Gemma's §3 requires distributors to make the use restrictions **enforceable in their own downstream agreements** and to hand every third-party recipient a copy of the agreement ([Gemma Terms](https://open.windriver.com/info/uni-license-list/licenses/gemma-tou-2025-03-24.html)) — the same pass-through obligation already rejected. Moondream 3.x is BSL 1.1 / bespoke *and* requires Ampere or newer, excluding the T4 twice over. The clean set is small and worth naming: **Qwen3-VL-2B/4B, InternVL3.5-2B, SmolVLM2-2.2B, Florence-2 (MIT), moondream2, PP-OCRv5 (Apache-2.0), dots.ocr 1.0 (MIT)**. Two flags: Qwen3-VL-**2B**'s Apache-2.0 status is corroborated by four third-party sources but **was not confirmed from the official repo**, and dots.ocr drifted from MIT at 1.0 to bespoke supplementary terms at 1.5 while disappearing from Hugging Face once already — mirror anything you depend on.

Here is the collision the two notes create, and it must not be averaged away: **the model that most improves the A10G economics is the model with the documented Turing serving failure.** Issue #29743 is a Qwen3-VL report — `qwen3_vl.py` rejects every attention backend outside FLASH_ATTN / TORCH_SDPA / ROCM_AITER_FA, FA2 needs SM80+, and the working combination (`VLLM_ATTENTION_BACKEND=TRITON_ATTN` plus the then-undocumented `--mm-encoder-attn-backend TORCH_SDPA`) was user-archaeology, not a fix. Swapping to Qwen3-VL-4B on the A10G is close to free and probably accuracy-positive. Swapping to Qwen3-VL-4B *on a T4* buys a $5/month saving in exchange for an unowned serving path. Take the first; the second has to earn itself with a measurement.

## Index scale, not photograph realism, is what breaks the cheap tier

The training-set research inverts the intuitive worry. The question people ask is "can we simulate photographs well enough from clean renders?" The measured answer is that photographic realism is worth a few points while **index size is worth tens**. In the one study that is exactly this problem — 404 phone photographs of 101 book covers matched against clean catalogue covers — top-1 with no distractors reached VLAD 0.68 and OCR 0.65; adding **104,132 Open Library distractor covers** collapsed VLAD to **0.18** while OCR fell only to **0.31** ([Shih et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1411.5307)). Visual matching degrades catastrophically with catalogue size because covers share visual patterns; text degrades gracefully because titles are nearly unique. The same monotone decay survives into 2026 hardware: ILIAS reports DINOv3-L at 31.1 mAP@1k with 5M distractors and 26.5 at 100M ([ILIAS](https://vrg.fel.cvut.cz/ilias/)).

**The consequence for the current instrument is blunt: a 122-photograph corpus with no distractor index cannot detect this failure mode at all**, because the failure is a function of index size and not of photograph quality. The expensive, currently unbudgeted item in any specialisation plan is not photography and not a renderer — it is a distractor index at realistic scale.

A second reversal: the ethics constraint costs the retrieval path far less than it appears. The Met dataset trains a **224,408-class cross-domain recogniser with literally zero photographs in training** — contrastive learning on clean catalogue images alone, with crop, colour-jitter and grayscale augmentation — and answers visitor photographs at 36.1 GAP / 55.0 ACC ([The Met](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2202.01747)). Photographs were needed only to *measure*. And the value of elaborate photo-simulation shrinks as backbones improve: DIHE's learned restyler gave +0.153 1-NN with a small encoder and only **+0.030 with a pretrained VGG-16** ([DIHE](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1902.00760)). Measure the frozen baseline before building a renderer.

So **the training set a specialised retrieval tier needs is mostly free, and photographs serve only three roles**, in descending value per shot: evaluation and threshold calibration (what the existing corpus already is); a few hundred *unlabelled* real photographs to characterise the photographic domain (DIHE used **547 unlabelled crops**, no identity labels, ethically trivial); and class-agnostic detector boxes (Tonioni needed **1,247 box-labelled images**, boxes only, no product identity — [arXiv 1810.01733](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1810.01733)). Hard negatives cost nothing: mine the current embedding's top-10 as the Met does, and scale the triplet margin by bibliographic hierarchy as DIHE does — series, imprint, publisher, author are free from catalogue metadata. One book-specific hazard has no analogue in the grocery literature: **two ISBNs of the same work frequently share identical cover art**, so they are not negatives at all, and the retrieval target must be the *work* or the *cover art*, with edition disambiguation left to the barcode and text paths. Decide that before generating any data, or the negatives are poisoned.

The project's own capture economics are the best labour datapoint available: 122 images shot to date covering **~45–55 distinct books**, with **19 of 122 requiring a follow-up round** before they could carry a scoring label, and `expect_isbn` still a single-valued field that cannot express a multi-book frame ([CORPUS-INTAKE.md](/Users/erinversfeld/thestacks/images/CORPUS-INTAKE.md); [SHOT-LIST.md](/Users/erinversfeld/thestacks/images/SHOT-LIST.md)). Budget a ~15% label-completion second pass; encode the ISBN-13 in the filename at capture time.

For the *generative* path the answer is different and simpler: **it needs no training set at all.** It needs a prompt, an evaluation corpus, and the ISBN gate — and that is fortunate, because Baek's scene-text result says real transcription labels beat synthetic ones at roughly **58:1** (276K real images, ~1.7% of 16M synthetic, matched or surpassed them — [CVPR 2021](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/CVPR2021/html/Baek_What_if_We_Only_Use_Real_Datasets_for_Scene_Text_CVPR_2021_paper.html)), and there is no ethical route to 276K real cover-text crops. Use a reader someone else trained on public data; do not train one.

What specialisation *is* worth doing is narrower than "a specialised cover model" and much cheaper. The baseline's named failures — reading the author instead of the title, reading blurb text — are **selection** failures, not recognition failures, and a fine-tune installs an output convention without requiring any new visual capability. That is the strongest argument for specialisation here, and it is an argument from task structure, not from a cited result. Treat the headline claim sceptically in both directions: LoRA Land found fine-tuned 7B models beat fine-tuned 2B models on **29 of 31 tasks** ([LoRA Land](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2405.00732)), and a Manchu-script OCR study found a LoRA-tuned 7B at 87.5% beating a LoRA-tuned 3B at 84.4% despite the 3B training for more epochs ([arXiv 2507.06761](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.06761v1)). Specialisation reliably beats *not* specialising at every size; it does not reliably substitute for parameters. But the comparison the project actually faces — tuned-small versus **untuned** 7B — is the one the literature barely covers, and every datapoint addressing it points favourably. Note also olmOCR's methodological warning: its authors **abandoned LoRA for full fine-tuning** because LoRA gave higher loss ([olmOCR](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.18443)); at 2–4B, full fine-tuning still fits one A100 and costs hours, not an order of magnitude. Published QLoRA runs on 7B text models land at 2–4 hours on a single A100 at ~$1.19–1.48/hr — **under $20**, one month's budget paid once. Every one of those cost figures is a text-LLM analogue from vendor blogs and none models image-token overhead, which for a cover task is the dominant term; a short measured run is the only reliable estimate.

## No book-cover dataset licenses its pixels — anywhere

This is a negative finding, stated as such, and it is the hard stop on building a cover-identity training set at scale. **Open Library publishes no licence for the cover images at all.** Its CC0 reputation attaches to the bibliographic *metadata dumps*; the covers are community uploads of publisher artwork, and the API page says plainly **"The covers API is intended for displaying covers on public facing websites and not for bulk download"** and **"Please, do not crawl our cover API"** ([Covers API](https://openlibrary.org/dev/docs/api/covers)). Bulk tar files exist on archive.org, but availability is a distribution fact, not a grant — and the Internet Archive explicitly "does not make guarantees as to the copyright status of items" and pushes the rights determination onto the downloader ([IA rights](https://help.archive.org/help/rights/)). **Google Books is worse**: the general Google APIs ToS forbids "Scrape, build databases, or otherwise create permanent copies of such content" and "create a derivative work of", with unilateral modification on 30 days' notice ([Google APIs ToS](https://developers.google.com/terms)) — the same mutable-terms objection already raised against DINOv3, plus a content-removal demand right. **Amazon is a contractual rule-out**: PA-API permits storing a *link* to an image for up to 24 hours and no caching of the image itself (second-hand via a clause archive, corroborated by Amazon's own [1-day image cache timeout](https://webservices.amazon.com/paapi5/documentation/best-programming-practices.html) — re-read the live agreement before relying on the exact wording). The canonical academic set, **uchidalab's 207,572-book corpus, asserts "fair use for academic purposes" over Amazon-hosted, Amazon-copyright images** ([uchidalab](https://github.com/uchidalab/book-dataset)) — a self-assessment, not a licence, and one that does not survive productisation.

There are **two stacked traps**, not one. The familiar one is the annotations/pixels split: COCO's annotations are CC BY 4.0 while image use "is governed by the Flickr Terms of Use"; Open Images states it honestly — "The annotations are licensed by Google LLC under CC BY 4.0" and "The images are listed as having a CC BY 2.0 license", with an explicit no-warranty disclaimer ([Open Images V7](https://storage.googleapis.com/openimages/web/factsfigures_v7.html)). The second trap is specific to this task and is the one that closes the door: **a photograph of a bookshelf embeds copyrighted cover artwork as a derivative work**, so a photographer's CC grant cannot reach the publisher's copyright in the cover design. Wikimedia Commons confirms this is a real distinction rather than an over-reading — it refuses "Scans or reproductive photographs of copyrighted artwork, especially book covers" outright ([Commons:Licensing](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Commons:Licensing)).

What *is* legally obtainable splits cleanly along the line the architecture already draws:

| Use | Licensed source | Terms |
|---|---|---|
| Photographic conditions (geometry, glare, blur, occlusion, shelf context) | **Open Images V7**, ~9M images with a `Book` class | Images CC BY 2.0, annotations CC BY 4.0 |
| Scene-text reading | **TextOCR** (28,134 images), **HierText** (11,639) | CC BY 2.0 pixels inherited; HierText's SA is viral |
| Spine detection/segmentation | **4TU book-spine set**, 661 images + polygons | **CC0** — unconditional, redistributable |
| Historic typography, binding diversity | British Library / Flickr Commons, Project Gutenberg | PD by age; "no known copyright restrictions" is a rights statement, not a licence |
| **Modern cover artwork at scale** | **None** | — |

Flagged under the DINOv3 standard and not used: Unsplash (terminable on immediate notice, redistribution forbidden, "internal business purposes" only), YFCC100M (a signed Webscope agreement layered over per-image CC), Open Shelves (CC BY-NC-SA), ABO (CC BY-NC on the better-sourced reading). ICDAR / Robust Reading licences could not be established at all — treat as unusable until the per-competition registration terms are read.

The conclusion this forces is also the one the technical evidence independently reaches: **learn conditions from licensed photography, keep identity in the catalogue at runtime.** Open Library covers as a *runtime lookup and a locally-held, never-redistributed evaluation index* is defensible — it is exactly what the API says it is for. The same covers as redistributed training data are not. That leaves catalogue-side contrastive training (the Met's Phase-2-shaped, highest-value, zero-photograph step) legally **unresolved rather than cleared** — the research flags it as a genuine blocker, not a detail, and it needs a decision before any data is generated. The one route to licensed modern cover artwork is not a dataset at all: it is a publisher or bookshop partner granting rights it actually holds, which maps onto the existing partner-integration model and is a business arrangement rather than a data source. One further decision the literature cannot make: whether a Creative-Commons web photo corpus containing bystanders is acceptable as an *evaluation distractor index*, given that the project's stated reason for its no-user-uploads rule — permanence and absent consent — applies to that use too.

## JEPA is a pretraining research programme, not a deployment option

Three independent disqualifiers, any one sufficient. **Licence:** I-JEPA and V-JEPA 1 ship CC-BY-NC 4.0 for both code and weights, granting use "for NonCommercial purposes only" with automatic termination on breach ([ijepa LICENSE](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/facebookresearch/ijepa/main/LICENSE); [ijepa_vith14_1k](https://huggingface.co/facebook/ijepa_vith14_1k)). This is not the DINOv3 failure mode — CC-BY-NC is standard, irrevocable and well understood — it simply withholds the permission that matters. **Evidence:** I-JEPA is evaluated on **zero retrieval benchmarks in its own paper** (seven tables, all classification, probing and Clevr counting) and does not appear as a baseline in DINOv2's instance-recognition table. That absence is the finding: retrieval requires no training, is cheap to run, and is the standard showcase for an off-the-shelf-features claim. The nearest architectural cousin, MAE — the other no-view-augmentation method — scores **2.2 mAP on Oxford-Hard where DINOv2 ViT-B/14 scores 49.5**, and 4.2 vs 45.6 on AmsterTime ([DINOv2 Table 9](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2304.07193v2)). I-JEPA should sit above MAE and well below the view-invariant family; this is an inference, but the burden of proof sits with anyone proposing it. Its single worst transfer result is on **iNat18, the fine-grained benchmark** (47.6 vs iBOT's 57.3 despite being far larger) — and book-cover matching is fine-grained instance retrieval, where two Penguin Modern Classics differ only by title typography on identical livery. **Size:** the smallest released I-JEPA checkpoint is **~600M params with no base or small variant at all**, roughly 7× SigLIP 2 ViT-B/16's 86M, and no inference latency has ever been published for any JEPA checkpoint on any hardware.

V-JEPA 2 escapes the licence problem — **MIT for code, MIT declared on the checkpoint cards**, with an 80M ViT-B/16 in the 2.1 release ([vjepa2](https://github.com/facebookresearch/vjepa2)) — and still fails. It is a *clip* encoder: the ViT-L checkpoint consumes **64 frames per clip**, and clip embeddings deliberately pool away exactly the per-object detail that distinguishes one spine from its neighbour. Its entire headline result set is temporal and physical — action anticipation, SSv2, robot manipulation — and its repo ships eval folders for action anticipation and classification only. The word "retrieval" appears in its intended-use text with no number anywhere behind it. For the video path, the relevant precedent runs the other way: DINOv2's image-trained frozen features linearly transfer to video classification (K400 73.2, UCF-101 89.1 for ViT-B/14) "even though our features were not trained on videos". **Sample frames, run the image encoder, aggregate votes** — one encoder serving both the still-upload and video paths, which is what $50/month requires.

One correction to the counterfactual, drawn from across the notes. The JEPA research nominates DINOv2 ViT-B/14 as the licence-clean default on the strength of Oxford/Paris. At realistic index scale the ranking flips: ILIAS puts **SigLIP2-L@512 at 25.3 mAP@1k against DINOv2-L's 18.5** at 5M distractors, and — the decisive part — linear adaptation on UnED gives **SigLIP2-L +12.0 and DINOv3-L only +1.8, DINOv2-L only +0.3**. An adapted SigLIP2-L lands near 37, above DINOv3-L's unadapted 31.1. **The DINOv3 licence rejection therefore costs far less than the raw leaderboard implies, because the licence-clean model is the one that responds to adaptation.** Flag the caveats: ILIAS lists UnED adaptation training data at 2.8M images (its own licence unexamined here), and the supporting fine-grained study reporting SigLIP2 at 94.26 R@10 vs DINOv2's 91.33 — with fusion reaching 97.02 — comes from a search snippet rather than a full-text read. That fusion result nonetheless agrees with the 2014 book paper's central lesson, which is the most reliable architectural claim in this entire body of research: two modalities that **fail differently** are worth more combined than either alone (0.60 top-1 at 104k distractors, against 0.31 for OCR and 0.18 for visual).

## Conclusion

The research reframes the decision rather than answering it as posed. The T4 question assumes the GPU is where the money is; the arithmetic says the GPU is worth about $5/month, bought with 1.7× latency, a silent numerical failure mode, a serving stack whose Turing VLM support is explicitly unowned, and a mandatory re-measurement of the 91% baseline because the T4 cannot run the checkpoint that produced it. Every one of those costs is incurred *before* the first saving arrives. The same money comes out of the system more cheaply, more safely and with a plausible accuracy gain by changing two numbers on the hardware already in place: the model (Qwen3-VL-4B, Apache-2.0, half the weights, 63.70% to the baseline's 41.8% on the one comparable scale) and the pixel budget (`max_pixels` at 1,003,520). That is the whole recommendation, and it fits in an afternoon's measurement against the existing corpus.

The deeper reframe concerns specialisation. The instinct — fine-tune a small model on book covers — is blocked twice, from directions that look unrelated and are not. Legally, there is no corpus of modern cover artwork anyone can grant, and the only route to one is a publisher relationship. Technically, the thing a cover-specialised retriever would be fighting is catalogue size, which no amount of training data or photographic realism touches, and which the project's current 122-image corpus is structurally incapable of detecting. Both constraints push toward the same architecture: **learn conditions from licensed photography, read text with a model someone else trained on public data, resolve identity against the catalogue at runtime, and let the ISBN gate be the verifier.** That is close to what already exists — which means the most valuable unmeasured number is not a model benchmark at all but the cascade's tier split, because it alone determines whether the expensive tier is hit often enough for any of this to matter.
