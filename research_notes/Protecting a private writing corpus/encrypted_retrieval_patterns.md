# Protecting a Sensitive RAG Corpus at Rest in Postgres: Encryption vs. Searchability Patterns

Scope: production patterns (current as of 2026) for a Postgres-based RAG stack — pgvector HNSW + optional tsvector FTS + AES-GCM application-layer encryption — holding one author's private writing, at hobby scale. Conclusions feed a schema/migration issue.

## What does a tsvector/GIN FTS index leak about sensitive source text, and what are the accepted patterns (vector-only, searchable encryption in production, trigram leakage)?

### Takeaway
A tsvector column/GIN index is effectively a lightly-lossy plaintext copy: stemmed lexemes plus word positions are stored and trivially extractable with built-in functions, so FTS over an encrypted column defeats the encryption. The patterns teams actually run are (a) skip FTS entirely for the sensitive corpus, (b) HMAC-blinded trigram/n-gram token indexes (SysReptor in production; CipherStash EQL as a product), each with acknowledged frequency-analysis and dictionary-probe leakage, or (c) tokenize-then-hash term arrays.

### Cited Findings
- A `tsvector` stores stemmed lexemes with integer position numbers (and optional weights); stop words, casing, and original word forms are discarded — the conversion is lossy but preserves lexeme order via positions — [PostgreSQL docs: Text Search Features](https://www.postgresql.org/docs/current/textsearch-features.html)
- Extraction of the stored material is built in: `unnest(tsvector)` "expands a tsvector to a set of rows," returning each lexeme with its positions and weights, and `tsvector_to_array` converts a tsvector to an array of lexemes — [PostgreSQL docs: Text Search Features](https://www.postgresql.org/docs/current/textsearch-features.html)
- Standard column encryption makes text search impractical; the naive workaround is decrypt-every-row-then-LIKE, which the tutorial itself warns "can expose sensitive data to risks if not handled securely" — [dev.to: partial match on an encrypted column](https://dev.to/asifroyal/in-postgres-perform-a-partial-match-on-an-encrypted-column-value-5bal)
- SysReptor (pentest-reporting product) rejected three designs for searching encrypted project data: decrypting everything per search, disabling encryption, and keeping a plaintext search index beside the ciphertext — [SysReptor: Searching encrypted project data](https://docs.sysreptor.com/insights/project-search)
- SysReptor's shipped design: normalize text (Unicode NFC, case folding, whitespace collapse), split into UTF-8 trigrams, HMAC-SHA256 each trigram keyed with the application's encryption key, truncate to 16 bytes; "Only those 16-byte tokens are stored, not the trigrams themselves." Search re-tokenizes the term and does a containment check — "analogous to what `pg_trgm` does on plaintext" — [SysReptor: Searching encrypted project data](https://docs.sysreptor.com/insights/project-search)
- SysReptor's acknowledged leakage: the HMAC key is shared across projects, so identical trigrams produce identical tokens — "An attacker can see which projects share content, though not the content itself" (frequency analysis); anyone holding the key can compute tokens for guessed terms (dictionary probes, equivalent to key compromise); false positives when a term's trigrams are spread across different words — [SysReptor: Searching encrypted project data](https://docs.sysreptor.com/insights/project-search)
- CipherStash EQL (searchable-encryption product over standard Postgres) offers four encrypted index types: equality via "an HMAC-keyed hash" (covers `WHERE =`, `GROUP BY`, `UNIQUE`), match via "Bloom-filtered n-grams for free-text search" ("closer in spirit to `tsearch` than to SQL `LIKE`"), ORE (Order-Revealing Encryption) for range/`ORDER BY`, and `ste_vec` for JSONB containment; "Plaintext never crossed the wire to Postgres" — [CipherStash: Searchable encryption in Postgres](https://cipherstash.com/llms/md/blog/searchable-encryption-in-postgres)
- EQL's acknowledged limits: Bloom filters "trade a small false-positive rate for index size"; no leading-wildcard/positional patterns; match is "unsuitable for paragraph-scale text, such as searching a full novel" (directly relevant: a writing corpus is paragraph-scale); the guide does not discuss access-pattern or query-pattern leakage of its equality/ORE indexes — [CipherStash: Searchable encryption in Postgres](https://cipherstash.com/llms/md/blog/searchable-encryption-in-postgres)
- Alibaba Cloud documents the tokenize-then-irreversibly-hash pattern: convert strings to tokens, irreversibly encrypt the tokens, store in arrays so fuzzy/full-text queries still work — [Alibaba Cloud: encrypted full-text search](https://www.alibabacloud.com/blog/support-for-encryption-full-text-search-and-fuzzy-query-of-multi-language-strings_595638)
- A PostgresConf 2025 session frames pgvector embeddings themselves as "a potential source of information leakage" and proposes keeping values hidden from DBAs — [PostgresConf 2025: Secure semantic search with pgvector](https://postgresconf.org/conferences/postgresconf_global_2025/program/proposals/secure-semantic-search-with-pgvector)
- No found source quantifies what searchable indexes reveal (frequency/access-pattern leakage rates); blind indexes and n-gram Bloom filters "generally leak some information, so you should check the scheme's threat model" — synthesis across [CipherStash docs](https://cipherstash.com/docs/stack/platform/searchable-encryption) and [SysReptor](https://docs.sysreptor.com/insights/project-search)

### Inferences
- For prose, tsvector leakage is near-total in practice: ordering extracted lexemes by position yields a stemmed, stop-word-stripped transcript of the author's writing — enough to reconstruct meaning, style, names, and plot. A GIN index additionally persists those lexemes as index keys in index pages, which travel into base backups and WAL. Treat "tsvector over the corpus" as "plaintext corpus."
- A plaintext `pg_trgm` index is worse than tsvector for leakage (raw character trigrams, no stemming loss); the only acceptable trigram variant for sensitive text is SysReptor's *blinded* (HMAC-keyed) form, and even that leaks cross-document similarity under one key.
- For a single-author private corpus, the frequency-analysis leakage SysReptor accepts (which documents share content) is lower-stakes than for multi-tenant data — the main residual risks are dictionary probes (requires the key, i.e., already app compromise) and the operational complexity of a bespoke blind index.
- The decision gradient for this stack: vector-only retrieval (no tsvector/trgm on corpus text) is the zero-leakage, zero-new-machinery default; an HMAC-blinded trigram token table is the proven DIY upgrade if keyword search is later demanded; CipherStash EQL is the buy option but is overkill at hobby scale and explicitly weak for paragraph-scale text.

### Gaps
- No academic or practitioner source quantifying reconstruction fidelity from a stolen tsvector/GIN index specifically (the capability is self-evident from the docs' function set, but no measured study was found).
- No found example of a team running hybrid FTS+vector retrieval over an encrypted corpus; hybrid-search guidance found assumes plaintext.

## What does application-layer AEAD encryption (Cloak/ActiveRecord-encryption-style) protect against in practice, what does it not, and what is sane key management at small scale?

### Takeaway
App-layer AES-GCM protects the data wherever the database's bytes go — dumps, backups, SQL-injection reads, over-privileged DB roles, provider-side access — because only ciphertext ever reaches Postgres; it does not protect against app-server compromise or key exfiltration, since the app holds the key and decrypts at runtime. Small-scale practice: one 256-bit key in an env-injected secret (CLOAK_KEY pattern), ciphertexts self-tagged with key identity, rotation = add new key + lazy re-encrypt on read + batch task to retire the old key.

### Cited Findings
- cloak_ecto's default cipher is `Cloak.Ciphers.AES.GCM` with a 256-bit key ("relatively strong encryption 256-bit encryption key encoded with Base64"), configured as `default: {Cloak.Ciphers.AES.GCM, tag: "AES.GCM.V1", key: Base.decode64!(...)}`; the recommended production pattern reads the key from an environment variable in the vault's `init/1` callback (`key: decode_env!("CLOAK_KEY")`); encrypted fields are `:binary` columns — [cloak_ecto install guide](https://cloak-ecto.hexdocs.pm/install.html)
- Cloak's own scope statement: "cloak encrypts data at rest in the database. The data in your Ecto structs at runtime is not encrypted"; it inherits Erlang `:crypto`'s security; its Ecto types "do not support user-specific encryption keys" — [Cloak hexdocs](https://hexdocs.pm/cloak/readme.html) (as summarized from hexdocs search results)
- Cloak key rotation: every ciphertext is tagged with the key that produced it ("this makes it easy to tell whether a ciphertext was produced under the old or new key"), old and new keys coexist in config, data "will gradually be converted over to the new key as they are used" (lazy re-encrypt on read), and `mix cloak.migrate.ecto` proactively re-encrypts all rows so the old key can be decommissioned — [cloak_ecto: How to Rotate Keys](https://hexdocs.pm/cloak_ecto/rotate_keys.html), [mix cloak.migrate.ecto](https://hexdocs.pm/cloak_ecto/Mix.Tasks.Cloak.Migrate.Ecto.html)
- Rails Active Record Encryption (the closest mainstream analogue) states what it protects: "if someone gains access to your application logs or database backup, the encrypted data remains unreadable" and it "helps avoid accidental exposure of sensitive information in your application console or logs" — [Rails guide: Active Record Encryption](https://guides.rubyonrails.org/active_record_encryption.html)
- The Rails guide presents no formal threat model and never states what happens if keys are exposed; keys live in credentials/env, so key holders decrypt everything (guide's own setup, inference flagged by the fetched summary) — [Rails guide](https://guides.rubyonrails.org/active_record_encryption.html)
- Rails on querying: non-deterministic (random-IV AES-GCM) columns cannot be queried by value ("not possible"); deterministic mode (IV = HMAC-SHA256 of plaintext) enables equality only, and "the determinism makes crypto-analysis easier"; "non-deterministic encryption is recommended for all data unless you need to query by the encrypted attribute"; "Rotating keys is not supported for deterministic encryption" — [Rails guide](https://guides.rubyonrails.org/active_record_encryption.html)
- Rails envelope option at small scale: `EnvelopeEncryptionKeyProvider` generates a random data key per encryption operation, encrypts it with the primary key, and stores it alongside the data; default key derivation is PBKDF2 from a password; recommended minimums "12 bytes for the primary key and 20 bytes for the salt" — [Rails guide](https://guides.rubyonrails.org/active_record_encryption.html)
- Rails operational details worth copying: encrypted attributes are "automatically filtered out of the Rails logs"; "an encrypted column can take up to four times its clear version size" — [Rails guide](https://guides.rubyonrails.org/active_record_encryption.html)
- cloak_ecto equality lookup pattern: a mirrored `_hash` column using an HMAC type (`Cloak.Ecto.HMAC`), queried as `Repo.get_by(User, email_hash: ...)` — i.e., a blind index, with the same determinism leakage class as Rails deterministic mode — [cloak_ecto install guide](https://cloak-ecto.hexdocs.pm/install.html)
- GitLab's key-rotation design doc frames the operational risks: "In the event of a key compromise, customers would face service disruption during key rotation" — rotation readiness is part of the threat model, not an afterthought — [GitLab handbook: Encryption keys rotation](https://handbook.gitlab.com/handbook/engineering/architecture/design-documents/encryption_keys_rotation/)
- CipherStash's critique of relying on provider at-rest encryption alone: "encryption-at-rest does not help — the data is decrypted as soon as it's queried"; disk/volume encryption protects against stolen disks, column-level app encryption protects against database-level exposure — [CipherStash: AI and RAG](https://cipherstash.com/docs/solutions/ai-and-rag)
- Vendor-neutral small-scale key guidance: store keys in a separate KMS (AWS KMS, Vault), "not on the same server as the data," and encrypt backups "since exports are often unencrypted" — [twig.so: Vector DB encryption / key rotation](https://help.twig.so/rag-scenarios-and-solutions/privacy/key-rotation) (secondary blog-style source)

### Inferences
- Concrete protects/does-not-protect ledger for this stack: **protects** — Neon console/DB-viewer reads, pg_dump theft, backup/branch/snapshot theft, SQL-injection `SELECT` (attacker gets ciphertext binaries), any Postgres role with table SELECT, provider insider/compromise at the storage layer. **Does not protect** — compromise of the Fly.io app VM (key in env + plaintext in BEAM memory), exfiltration of CLOAK_KEY from secrets/config/CI, anything the app legitimately decrypts and then logs/caches/sends onward.
- At hobby scale, the Cloak single-key + tagged-ciphertext model already is the envelope pattern's poor cousin and is adequate: the key never touches the DB, rotation is mechanically supported, and there is no KMS bill. The one discipline that matters is that CLOAK_KEY lives only in the runtime secret store (Fly secrets / `.env` locally, per this project's existing convention) and never in repo, logs, or the database host.
- Prefer non-deterministic AEAD for corpus chunk text (no equality queries needed on prose); reserve HMAC blind-index columns for the rare exact-match need (e.g., dedup by normalized title), accepting the determinism leakage only there.
- Plan the migration with 2–4x column growth (Rails' figure) for the encrypted `:binary` chunk-text column.

### Gaps
- No dedicated published threat-model document for cloak/cloak_ecto exists; its guarantees must be assembled from install/rotation docs plus the Erlang crypto inheritance statement.
- No found incident write-up measuring real-world outcomes (e.g., breach where app-layer encryption did or didn't save the data) specific to this pattern.

## How do app-layer encryption, Postgres RLS, and least-privilege roles layer — and what does a hosted-Postgres (Neon) provider-side compromise expose?

### Takeaway
The layers answer different attackers: roles/GRANTs stop other database principals, RLS stops the app's own bugs from crossing row boundaries, app-layer encryption stops everyone who sees the database's bytes without the app key — including the provider. Neon encrypts storage with AES-256 under AWS KMS/Azure Key Vault keys *it* manages, so a provider-side compromise (or any credentialed read) sees your plaintext columns; Neon's own docs route column confidentiality to pgcrypto or application-side encryption and leave RLS to you.

### Cited Findings
- Neon: "all customer and sensitive data is encrypted using AES-256 encryption at rest," keys "managed through AWS KMS and Azure Key Vault"; TLS 1.2+ in transit; the Neon Proxy fronts connections; IP allowlists (Scale plan) and AWS PrivateLink available — [Neon: Security overview](https://neon.com/docs/security/security-overview)
- Neon's HIPAA guide: same AES-256 storage-layer encryption with AWS KMS-managed keys; when HIPAA is enabled Neon configures pgaudit logging, "while you must handle Row-Level Security" — [Neon: HIPAA-compliant applications](https://neon.com/guides/hipaa-compliant-applications)
- Neon's at-rest encryption is transparent to any credentialed reader: a community answer notes that reading data back in a DB viewer shows plaintext; "Encrypting columns in the application before writing them gives ciphertext in the database" — [Answer Overflow thread](https://www.answeroverflow.com/m/1214693000798806016) (community, not official Neon)
- Neon documents in-database column encryption via pgcrypto (`pgp_sym_encrypt` etc.) as the provider-sanctioned column-confidentiality option — [Neon: pgcrypto extension](https://neon.tech/docs/extensions/pgcrypto)
- CipherStash markets app-side encryption specifically as the answer to "encrypt PII in Neon without managing your own keys," i.e., the recognized posture for keeping plaintext out of a hosted Postgres entirely — [CipherStash: encrypt PII in Neon](https://cipherstash.com/llms/md/solutions/how-do-you-encrypt-pii-in-neon)
- SysReptor's layering statement of the same posture: "The encryption key is held by the application server, not the database," so the database only ever stores ciphertext — [SysReptor](https://docs.sysreptor.com/insights/project-search)
- OWASP LLM08 on the access-control layer for retrieval stores: "Implement fine-grained access controls and permission-aware vector and embedding stores" and "Ensure strict logical and access partitioning of datasets in the vector database" — [OWASP LLM08:2025](https://genai.owasp.org/llmrisk/llm082025-vector-and-embedding-weaknesses/)
- CipherStash's framing of what each layer buys: provider at-rest encryption ≈ stolen-disk protection; app-layer column encryption ≈ protection "against database-level exposure" — [CipherStash: AI and RAG](https://cipherstash.com/docs/solutions/ai-and-rag)

### Inferences
- Provider-compromise exposure matrix for Neon specifically: storage-layer AES-256 is decrypted by Neon's own KMS-held keys on every page read, so anything Neon's control plane (or an attacker inside it, or a subpoena) can read includes: plaintext of unencrypted columns, every pgvector embedding, every tsvector/GIN index, all WAL, and all branches/snapshots. App-layer-encrypted columns are the only columns that survive this as ciphertext.
- "The app can read, nothing else can" decomposes as: (1) one dedicated login role for the app with table-level GRANTs only on what it serves (this project already runs `stacks_app`/`stacks_readonly`/`stacks_dbt` role separation); (2) RLS policies as the guard against application-logic bugs leaking rows across users — noting this project's own finding that local tests connecting as superuser bypass RLS and GRANTs entirely, so the layer is untested unless probed with `SET LOCAL ROLE`; (3) app-layer AEAD as the only layer that holds against the provider and against any role that *does* have SELECT.
- RLS and roles contribute nothing against a provider-side or storage-level read — they are enforced by the very Postgres process the provider operates. Only the app-held key draws that boundary.
- For a single-author corpus, per-user keys (which Cloak cannot do via Ecto types anyway) buy nothing; a single corpus key scoped to the app is the right shape.

### Gaps
- Neon has no customer-managed-key (BYOK/CMEK) offering in any doc found; could not confirm whether one is planned. (Absence of evidence from the security overview and HIPAA guide.)
- No independent audit or incident report describing an actual hosted-Postgres provider-side compromise and what it exposed; the exposure analysis above is derived from the documented architecture, not from a case study.

## Do RAG teams encrypt chunk text and decrypt per-retrieval, what does it cost, and what are the caching/logging pitfalls?

### Takeaway
Yes — the documented production pattern is: embeddings stay plaintext for ANN search, chunk text (and sensitive metadata) is AEAD-encrypted app-side, and only the top-k retrieved rows are decrypted at prompt-assembly time. Direct latency numbers are scarce (vendor claim: "close to no-encryption performance"); the real reported hazard is downstream — decrypted chunks leaking into app logs, LLM-observability traces, tool spans, and error reports, which regex redaction provably does not fully catch.

### Cited Findings
- CipherStash's RAG pattern: "CipherStash encrypts sensitive fields in the application before they reach the database. The vector store can continue retrieving candidate rows while the application decrypts only the records selected for the prompt" — encrypt chunk + metadata, decrypt per-retrieval — [CipherStash: AI and RAG](https://cipherstash.com/docs/solutions/ai-and-rag), [CipherStash docs: AI/RAG use case](https://docs.cipherstash.com/docs/use-cases/ai-rag)
- CipherStash explicitly flags that encrypting source text does not neutralize the embedding: "Embeddings may leak information about the source, so they should be included in your threat model" — [CipherStash: AI and RAG](https://cipherstash.com/docs/solutions/ai-and-rag)
- CipherStash performance claims for its encrypted-index scheme: "close to no-encryption performance" for most workloads and ~"410,000× faster than fully homomorphic encryption," both citing its own benchmark repos (vendor claims, not independently verified) — [CipherStash: Searchable encryption in Postgres](https://cipherstash.com/llms/md/blog/searchable-encryption-in-postgres)
- A 2026 academic review of RAG security: current RAG encryption defenses are "mostly limited to the static storage phase, which prevents direct theft but fails to protect runtime data flows"; searching over ciphertext in vector stores remains an open research area — [Towards Secure RAG: review (arXiv 2603.21654)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.21654)
- OWASP LLM08 retrieval-side controls: "Maintain detailed immutable logs of retrieval activities to detect and respond promptly to suspicious behavior" — [OWASP LLM08:2025](https://genai.owasp.org/llmrisk/llm082025-vector-and-embedding-weaknesses/)
- LLM-observability SDKs redact narrowly by default: Grafana's sanitizer runs curated secret patterns before export, skips user messages unless opted in, does not detect names/addresses/IDs/phone numbers, and does not scan model responses — "Sensitive content in a model's output reaches traces unmodified" — [Grafana Cloud: PII and secrets redaction](https://grafana.com/docs/grafana-cloud/machine-learning/ai-observability/privacy-and-security/pii-and-secrets-redaction/)
- Prompts leak through more surfaces than the prompt log: "tool spans, HTTP debug messages, and error reports as well as app logs," so one data policy must govern all telemetry exports; build telemetry from operational facts rather than copying the request; never log the input when redaction fails; automated PII detection "can't guarantee it finds every sensitive item" (Presidio point reported via OneUptime) — [OneUptime: redacting sensitive LLM data](https://oneuptime.com/blog/post/2026-11-16-how-to-redact-sensitive-llm-data-across-prompt-logs-tool-spans-and-error-reports/view)
- SysReptor's cost-avoidance rationale for its token index applies to the decrypt path too: searches are lookups over precomputed tokens, "avoiding per-request decryption of every finding" — i.e., the expensive anti-pattern is decrypt-the-corpus-per-query, not decrypt-top-k — [SysReptor](https://docs.sysreptor.com/insights/project-search)
- Rails' operational mitigation for the logging pitfall at the ORM layer: encrypted attributes "automatically filtered out of the Rails logs," console shows `[FILTERED]` — [Rails guide: Active Record Encryption](https://guides.rubyonrails.org/active_record_encryption.html)

### Inferences
- No source reports measured per-retrieval decryption latency, but the shape of the work bounds it: AES-GCM with hardware AES (standard on all current server CPUs, and what Erlang `:crypto` uses) processes gigabytes/second, so decrypting top-k ≈ 5–20 chunks of ~1–2 KB is microseconds — orders of magnitude below the embedding call and LLM call in the same request. The latency argument against encrypting chunk text does not survive contact with the architecture; the real costs are operational (key handling, rotation, migration) and the loss of in-database text search.
- The pitfall list to engineer against, concretely: (1) Ecto/telemetry query logs — ciphertext binaries are safe to log, but any `inspect` of a decrypted struct is not (Cloak: "data in your Ecto structs at runtime is not encrypted"); (2) LLM-call logging/tracing — assembled prompts contain decrypted corpus text and traces ship it off-box by default; (3) error reports capturing request/prompt context; (4) any cache of assembled prompts or retrieved chunks (ETS, Redis, HTTP-layer) recreates a plaintext store outside the encryption boundary and outside erasure's reach.
- For this project: the RAG request path should treat decrypted chunk text like it already treats user PII under GDPR — excluded from `event_log`, audit, warehouse, and telemetry; prompt/response logging, if wanted for evals, needs its own consciously-designed encrypted store, not the default trace exporter.

### Gaps
- No published latency/throughput benchmark specifically for "AEAD-decrypt top-k chunks per RAG query" was found; the microsecond estimate above is inference from AES-GCM hardware throughput, not a citation.
- No found case study of a team's decrypted-chunk cache or trace leak post-mortem; the logging-pitfall guidance is preventive (vendor docs + practitioner guide), not incident-derived.

## What do backups, snapshots, and replicas expose — for both text and vectors — and how sensitive are the vectors themselves (embedding inversion)?

### Takeaway
Every byte Postgres stores travels intact into dumps, WAL, replicas, and (on Neon) copy-on-write branches and snapshots: app-layer ciphertext stays ciphertext everywhere, while plaintext columns, tsvector/GIN indexes, and pgvector embeddings are exposed everywhere. The embeddings are not a safe residue: inversion attacks recover 92% of 32-token inputs exactly against known encoders, and the corpus's encoder (an open-source model) is exactly the "attacker has the embedding model" setting the attacks assume — so a leaked vector column approximates a leaked corpus.

### Cited Findings
- Neon's storage model: AES-256 at rest with Neon-managed KMS keys covers volumes/backups at the storage layer only; any credentialed or provider-side read decrypts transparently (see section 3 citations) — [Neon: Security overview](https://neon.com/docs/security/security-overview)
- Backups/exports are a named weak point in vector-store practice: "encrypt backups, since exports are often unencrypted" — [twig.so: Vector DB encryption](https://help.twig.so/rag-scenarios-and-solutions/privacy/key-rotation)
- CipherStash's snapshot claim for app/field-level encryption: an attacker with a database snapshot "sees only ciphertext without the key" (for the encrypted columns) — [CipherStash: Searchable encryption in Postgres](https://cipherstash.com/llms/md/blog/searchable-encryption-in-postgres)
- vec2text (Morris et al., 2023): an iterative method that "iteratively corrects and re-embeds text" can "recover 92% of 32-token text inputs exactly" from dense embeddings; it also recovers "important personal information (full names)" from a clinical-notes dataset; demonstrated against state-of-the-art embedding models incl. OpenAI's text-embedding-ada-002 — [Text Embeddings Reveal (Almost) As Much As Text, arXiv:2310.06816](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.06816)
- Earlier baseline: Song & Raghunathan (2020) recovered 50–70% of tokens (unordered) from embeddings — as reported in [a UC thesis survey](https://escholarship.org/content/qt92k8b7p9/qt92k8b7p9.pdf) and the inversion literature
- Generative inversion (Li et al., 2023) produces ordered, "mostly coherent" text, "some... even verbatim text sequences from inputs" — [arXiv:2305.03010](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.03010.pdf)
- Inversion generalizes beyond English and beyond full training access: multilingual inversion ([arXiv:2401.12192](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2401.12192)); ALGEN needs only a small number of leaked text–embedding pairs, aligning victim embeddings to an attack space — [arXiv:2502.11308](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.11308v2)
- The attacks' shared precondition: "all attacks require either access to the embedding model or a set of leaked text-embedding pairs" — [survey via ALGEN/related work](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.11308v2)
- OWASP LLM08 names embedding inversion as a first-class risk for stored vectors: "Attackers can exploit vulnerabilities to invert embeddings and recover significant amounts of source information" — but its mitigations are access-control/partitioning/logging; it offers no at-rest-encryption control for the vectors themselves — [OWASP LLM08:2025](https://genai.owasp.org/llmrisk/llm082025-vector-and-embedding-weaknesses/)
- The 2026 secure-RAG review confirms no production-ready encrypted-ANN option: searching over ciphertext in vector stores "remains an open research area" — [arXiv 2603.21654](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.21654)

### Inferences
- Exposure map after app-layer encryption of chunk text only: pg_dump / logical replication / Neon branch / snapshot / physical replica all contain (a) chunk ciphertext — safe without CLOAK_KEY; (b) embeddings in plaintext — invertible to ~approximate text, since this project's encoder (bge-m3) is open-source, satisfying the attacker-has-the-encoder precondition; (c) any tsvector/GIN or pg_trgm index over corpus text — near-plaintext. (b) and (c) are why "we encrypted the text column" is not the end of the analysis.
- Practical hobby-scale posture for the vectors, given no usable encrypted-ANN scheme exists: accept that embeddings ≈ sensitive data; rely on the same access boundary as the rest of the DB (app role only, no warehouse/dbt export of the embedding or chunk tables, no analytics replica containing them); document the residual provider-side exposure explicitly rather than pretending the vector column is inert. Noise-addition defenses exist in the literature but trade retrieval quality and lack production-grade tooling.
- Neon-specific: branches are copy-on-write clones of the data — every preview/dev branch duplicates the corpus exposure surface; a corpus table should either be excluded from seeded/preview flows or previews should carry a separate (or empty) corpus. Snapshot/branch sprawl is the hosted-Postgres analogue of the forgotten backup tape.
- Erasure interaction (project-specific): ciphertext rows still count as personal data for GDPR purposes — deletion must remove the rows (and ideally rotate/destroy the key if the whole corpus is erased, giving crypto-shredding for free on stray backups).

### Gaps
- No source found measuring inversion quality specifically against bge-m3 1024-dim embeddings; the 92%-exact figure is for other encoders at 32 tokens, and fidelity on longer chunks (200–500 tokens) is not reported in the fetched material — directionally worse for exact recovery, but semantic/entity leakage persists.
- No found documentation of Neon branch/snapshot internals sufficient to say whether deleted-branch data is crypto-shredded or merely unreferenced; retention behavior of corpus bytes in Neon's storage layer after branch deletion is unverified.
