# Fine-tuning Memorization and Extraction Risk (LoRA/PEFT on a Small Private Corpus)

Scope: how much a LoRA adapter trained on one author's private writing can leak that writing — to users of the model or to an attacker holding the adapter weights — and what production practice does about it. Decision context: hobby-scale platform weighing RAG-only now vs LoRA later. Current as of November 2026. Note: much of the PEFT-memorization literature is 2024–2026 arXiv preprint work, not yet peer-reviewed unless marked otherwise.

## How does memorization scale with corpus size, duplication, epochs, and adapter rank — and is small-corpus verbatim extraction MORE likely than for pretraining data?

### Takeaway
Memorization grows log-linearly with model capacity, example duplication, and prompt-context length (the pretraining baseline), and in fine-tuning it grows with epochs, rank, and generation-style tasks. The dominant small-corpus fact: ~16–32 effective repetitions of a sequence are enough for full verbatim reproduction at sampling temperature, and a dozens-to-hundreds-document corpus trained for several epochs puts *every* document in that regime — so per-example extraction risk is materially higher than for a typical (non-duplicated) pretraining example. LoRA substantially dampens, but does not eliminate, this compared with full fine-tuning.

### Cited Findings
- Baseline scaling laws (pretraining): memorization grows log-linearly with (1) model capacity, (2) number of times an example is duplicated, and (3) tokens of context used to prompt; "memorization is more prevalent than previously believed and will likely worsen as models scale" — [Carlini et al., Quantifying Memorization Across Neural Language Models, 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.07646)
- Deduplicating C4 cut memorization roughly tenfold in a GPT-2 model trained for two epochs — [Undesirable Memorization in LLMs: A Survey](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.02650)
- Epochs: extractability of fine-tuning data rose with each fine-tuning epoch in code models, and one epoch was already enough to memorize a large share of the attack set; duplication rate strongly affects extractability in fine-tuning as in pretraining — [How Much Do Code Language Models Remember?](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2501.17501). A Japanese-newspaper fine-tuning study likewise found memorization rose with epochs — [Quantifying Memorization... using Japanese Newspaper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2404.17143). A Llama 3 8B model fine-tuned 10 epochs retained more training information than a 1-epoch version — [Fragments to Facts](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.13819)
- Extended training and high learning rates produce "over-memorization": test accuracy holds while test perplexity rises, with reduced robustness and generation diversity — [Unveiling Over-Memorization in Finetuning LLMs](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.04117v1)
- Canary repetition threshold (LoRA fine-tune of LLaMA-2 7B on ~3,000 examples — i.e., exactly small-corpus scale): a canary repeated 16× was reproduced in full at temperature 0.1; repeated 32×, it was reproduced in full at every temperature tested (0.1, 0.5, 1.0) — [Savine, AI Safety: Memorization in Fine-Tuned LLMs, 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.21009v2)
- Adapter rank: membership-inference AUC rises with LoRA rank with diminishing returns (adapting W_V: rank 1 → 0.80, rank 2 → 0.82, rank 4 → 0.83; plateau around rank 50 with all attention matrices adapted). Value (W_V) and output (W_O) matrices contribute more to memorization than query/key (W_Q, W_K) at equal parameter count — [Savine 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.21009v2)
- LoRA target-module placement: LoRA on projection layers raised memorization more than on attention layers; applying it to both gave the highest memorization — [Impact of Fine-Tuning Methods on Memorization](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.00258)
- LoRA vs full fine-tuning (GPT-2 family + Llama 3.x, 10 epochs, corpora of 1k–10k samples): full fine-tuning's paraphrase-plagiarism rate exceeded 50% at duplication ρ=20 and near-perfect similarity matches appeared once ρ≥5; full fine-tuning's memorization rose with model scale and duplication. LoRA (rank 16) and head-only tuning stayed near zero on plagiarism metrics across all model sizes and duplication levels, with similarity distributions peaking ~0.4–0.5; on Llama 3.1 8B with LoRA fewer than 5 generated samples exceeded 0.8 similarity. Raising rank/α 4→32 slightly raised max similarity (0.70→0.75) but all maxima stayed below 0.8; dropout reduced similarity with little utility loss — [Wang & Li, Leaner Training, Lower Leakage, 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.20856)
- Task type: generation-heavy fine-tuning tasks (summarization, dialogue) show much more memorization than classification or translation — [Zeng et al., Exploring Memorization in Fine-tuned Language Models](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.06714v2); head-only fine-tuning memorizes most, full-model and bottleneck adapters lower but comparable — [same paper, PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.06714)
- Fuzzy duplicates: near-duplicates of a sequence contribute to its memorization almost as much as exact copies, so exact-match deduplication understates effective duplication — [The Mosaic Memory of LLMs](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.15523v2)

### Inferences
- An author's private corpus of dozens-to-hundreds of documents, fine-tuned for a typical 3–10 epochs, gives every document an effective repetition count in or near the 16–32 range that produced *complete verbatim canary reproduction* in the LLaMA-2/LoRA study — whereas a typical pretraining example is seen once and (per the Carlini scaling laws) is unlikely to be extractable without duplication. So yes: per-example verbatim extraction is more likely for a small fine-tuning corpus than for typical pretraining data, even though no paper states this head-to-head comparison numerically.
- Drafts and revisions of the same manuscript are fuzzy duplicates of each other; ingesting an author's working folder (multiple drafts of one piece) multiplies effective duplication far beyond document count — the Mosaic Memory result says exact-match dedup won't see this.
- A writing assistant is the worst-case task type: open-ended generation in the author's voice, which is exactly the memorization-prone regime (vs classification), and the stylistic goal actively rewards the model for reproducing the corpus's phrasing.
- Config choices that measurably lower risk if a LoRA is ever trained: low rank (≤8–16), adapters on attention (and especially *not* only W_V/W_O/projection layers), dropout, few epochs, aggressive near-duplicate dedup of drafts.

### Gaps
- No paper found gives a direct numerical extractability comparison of "same text in pretraining vs in a small fine-tune" on the same model; the conclusion above is inferred from the repetition/epoch findings.
- The two headline LoRA studies disagree in emphasis (Wang & Li: LoRA near-zero plagiarism and flat in duplication; Savine: LoRA-tuned model fully reproduces 16×-repeated canaries). Settings differ (metrics, corpus homogeneity, rank); no reconciliation study found. Treat "LoRA is low-risk" as configuration-dependent, not a guarantee.
- Corpus-*size* scaling specifically (dozens vs hundreds vs thousands of docs, holding epochs fixed) was not systematically varied in any paper found.

## Named extraction attacks applicable to fine-tuned/adapted models, and demonstrated success rates

### Takeaway
Three attack families apply: (1) prompt-based verbatim extraction (prefix→suffix completion), which succeeds against fine-tunes at the repetition counts a small corpus naturally hits; (2) divergence attacks that break an aligned model out of chat behavior (150× extraction-rate lift on ChatGPT); and (3) membership inference, where the state of the art against LoRA fine-tunes reaches AUC ≈0.92 — close to a reliable oracle for "was this passage in the private corpus".

### Cited Findings
- Divergence attack (Nasr, Carlini et al. 2023): asking ChatGPT (gpt-3.5-turbo) to repeat a word forever caused it to diverge and emit training data at 150× the normal rate; >10,000 unique verbatim memorized training examples recovered for ~$200 of queries; "contemporary alignment techniques are not sufficient to prevent regurgitation"; the specific attack was particular to ChatGPT among models tested — [Scalable Extraction of Training Data from (Production) Language Models](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.17035); secondary coverage claims >5% of output was verbatim training data in the strongest configuration — [HubSpot summary](https://blog.hubspot.com/ai/chatgpt-leaking-training-data) (secondary source; verify against the paper)
- Prompt-based (prefix) extraction against fine-tunes: in code models, giving the model a training-example prefix recovered suffixes at rates that grew per epoch, with substantial extraction after one epoch — [arXiv 2501.17501](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2501.17501); in the LoRA/LLaMA-2 small-corpus study, prefix-prompting fully recovered 32×-repeated sequences at all temperatures — [arXiv 2507.21009](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.21009v2)
- Membership inference vs fine-tuned LLMs: SPV-MIA (NeurIPS 2024) raised attack AUC from ~0.7 (prior methods) to ~0.9; average AUC 0.924 across three datasets and four LLMs with **LoRA as the default PEFT method**; average TPR@1%FPR of 46.9%; ~30% AUC improvement over the strongest baseline (LiRA-Candidate) — [Fu et al., Practical Membership Inference Attacks against Fine-tuned LLMs via Self-prompt Calibration](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.06062)
- A 2026 window-based MIA (WBC) reports average AUC 0.839 vs 0.754 for the strongest baseline (different method/settings, not directly comparable) — [arXiv 2601.02751](https://www.arxiv.org/pdf/2601.02751)
- A 2023 paper simulated a memorization attack on GPT-3 through OpenAI's fine-tuning API, demonstrating the attack surface exists on hosted fine-tuning products — [arXiv 2307.16382](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.16382?context=cs)
- Partial-information fragment inference: attackers holding fragments of a record can infer the rest from a fine-tuned model (studied on Llama 3 8B; 10-epoch model leaked more than 1-epoch) — [Fragments to Facts](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.13819)
- "LoRA as Oracle": task-specific LoRA adapters attached to a frozen model can themselves be used as probes to infer membership and backdoors without the original training data — [arXiv 2601.11207](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2601.11207)

### Inferences
- For unpublished writing, membership inference is itself a breach, not just a precursor: AUC ~0.9 means an attacker with API access can confirm with high confidence that a specific manuscript passage was in the author's private corpus (confirming authorship/possession of an unpublished text). The leak threshold is therefore lower than "verbatim extraction."
- The attacks are black-box (query access only). Anyone who can chat with the assistant is in the threat model, not just someone who steals weights.
- Combined attack path for a writing assistant: MIA to confirm corpus membership, then prefix-prompting with any publicly known fragment of the author's style/openings to extend into unpublished continuations (the Fragments-to-Facts pattern).

### Gaps
- No published end-to-end case study found of extracting a private *literary* corpus from a shipped LoRA adapter; evidence is from news, medical, code, and benchmark corpora.
- Success rates for divergence-style attacks against small open-weight models serving a LoRA (as a hobby platform would run) are not reported; the 150×/$200 numbers are for gpt-3.5-turbo and are explicitly model-specific.

## Mitigations with evidence, and how teams test an adapter for leakage before shipping

### Takeaway
Ordered by evidence strength: near-duplicate deduplication (≈10× reduction), minimal epochs/early stopping, low-rank + dropout + attention-only LoRA placement, then DP-SGD as the only *formal* guarantee — at real utility and compute cost, though LoRA+DP is the practical pairing and LoRA alone empirically lands near DP's privacy level. Pre-ship practice is an extraction eval: plant canaries during training, then audit with exposure/rank metrics, prefix-extraction probes, and MIA together — single metrics and weak canaries systematically underestimate risk. Output filters (exact/fuzzy match of generations against the corpus) are a backstop, not a fix.

### Cited Findings
- Deduplication: dedup of C4 cut memorization ~10× (GPT-2, 2 epochs) — [survey, arXiv 2410.02650](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.02650); near-duplicates must be included or risk is undercounted — [Mosaic Memory](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.15523v2)
- Epoch control/early stopping: per-epoch growth of extractability ([arXiv 2501.17501](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2501.17501)); over-memorization from extended training and high LR, detectable as rising test perplexity with flat accuracy ([arXiv 2508.04117](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.04117v1))
- LoRA configuration as mitigation: near-zero plagiarism-based memorization vs full fine-tuning at rank 16 with dropout ([Wang & Li](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.20856)); lower rank → lower MIA AUC, avoid adapting W_V/W_O only ([Savine](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.21009v2)); avoid projection-layer-only placement ([arXiv 2507.00258](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.00258))
- DP-SGD trade-offs: a 2025 head-to-head found full fine-tuning gives poor utility-privacy trade-offs; DP-SGD gives the best trade-offs but is computationally very expensive; **LoRA alone is "almost on par with DP in terms of utility and privacy" while much cheaper** — [Revisiting Privacy, Utility, and Efficiency Trade-offs](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.13313v2). Under DP, full fine-tuning and LoRA offer favorable trade-offs while prefix-tuning degrades severely; DP-LoRA still shows non-trivial utility drops (several points on GLUE-type benchmarks) — [RLDP](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.22565v1); DP-SGD "computationally demanding and often incurs substantial utility loss, especially for large models" — [TTLoRA-DP](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.10045v1)
- Non-DP PEFT already shows reduced leakage (lower MIA AUC) vs full fine-tuning, and PEFT is positioned as a complement when full DP fine-tuning is impractical — [Efficient and Private: Memorisation under DP PEFT](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2411.15831)
- LoRA is *not* inherently private despite its low-rank structure and randomness, though low-rank fine-tuning can be more private than full fine-tuning at a fixed noise level — [LoRA and Privacy](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.21719v1)
- Regularization: dropout on LoRA reduced similarity-based memorization with little utility loss — [Wang & Li](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.20856)
- Canary methodology: the exposure metric (rank of an inserted canary under the model) is the standard "canary extraction test" — [survey](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.02650). Common canary designs are weak auditors; better-designed canaries dramatically raise TPR@1%FPR, and weak canaries explain why frontier-lab canary studies report little memorization while extraction research recovers data from the same models — [Privacy Auditing of Large Language Models, ICLR 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.06808v1)
- Empirical privacy auditing is treated as a core pre-deployment evaluation (Google Research, 2026), including how LoRA rank and canary entropy affect memorization — [arXiv 2606.10481](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.10481v1)
- Single-metric audits underestimate exposure; combine perplexity, extraction probes, and MIA — [genomic canary study](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.08913); probe choice alone can flip a canary-memorization verdict, so audits should report several measures — [arXiv 2606.31168](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.31168); which context is loss-masked during SFT controls what gets memorized — [canaries under masked-context SFT](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.20656)

### Inferences
- A practical pre-ship extraction eval for a hobby-scale adapter: (1) insert several high-entropy canaries at varying repetition counts (1×, 4×, 16×) into the training set; (2) after training, measure canary exposure/rank, run prefix→suffix extraction over a sample of real corpus documents at several temperatures, and run an MIA (SPV-MIA-style) with held-out author text as non-members; (3) ship only if real-document suffix recovery is near zero and canary exposure at realistic repetition counts is low. All pieces are standard-tooling; nothing requires frontier-lab infrastructure.
- An output filter that checks generations against a fuzzy-match index of the corpus (e.g., n-gram or similarity threshold) maps directly onto the decision context's "the corpus may teach but never recite" rule — it is the enforcement backstop for exactly that policy, and the only mitigation that works after a bad adapter ships. But the Mosaic Memory result implies paraphrase-level leakage will pass an exact-match filter; the filter needs a similarity threshold, not string equality.
- At one-author scale, DP-SGD's ε accounting is awkward (the "unit of privacy" is one person who *is* the dataset), and the utility cost lands on exactly the stylistic fidelity a writing assistant exists for. The literature's own comparison (LoRA ≈ DP on empirical privacy at far lower cost) suggests the pragmatic stack is: dedup + few epochs + low-rank LoRA + dropout + extraction eval + output filter, with DP reserved for if/when the corpus includes third parties' personal data.

### Gaps
- No source found describing a named production team's actual pre-ship adapter leakage-testing pipeline (methodologies are from research papers, not engineering postmortems or vendor runbooks).
- No quantified utility cost of DP-SGD specifically at "hundreds of documents" scale; GLUE-style numbers are the closest proxy.

## Does RAG-only (frozen base, retrieval) eliminate the risk class, and what residual risks remain?

### Takeaway
RAG-only eliminates the weight-memorization risk class — nothing about the private corpus is in any trainable artifact — and one study even found retrieval *reduces* leakage of the base model's own training data. But it substitutes a different, well-attacked channel: the retrieved text sits verbatim in the prompt, and prompt-level extraction attacks have demonstrated up to ~84% coverage of a private RAG corpus; embeddings themselves are also invertible back to text. For a single-user private assistant the residual risk collapses to "who can query the assistant or read the vector store," which is an access-control problem rather than a model-behavior problem.

### Cited Findings
- Attackers can prompt a RAG system into reproducing its retrieval database; the same work found integrating retrieval can reduce leakage of the underlying LLM's training data — [The Good and The Bad: Exploring Privacy Issues in RAG, 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.16893)
- RAG-Thief: an automated agent with a feedback loop extracts private knowledge-base content at scale, ~3× the extraction ratio of prior state-of-the-art — [arXiv 2411.14110](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.14110v1)
- Treating extraction as an adaptive-coverage problem reaches up to 84.4% coverage of the RAG corpus within a fixed query budget (prior attacks stall after a few rounds) — [arXiv 2601.15678](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.15678v1)
- Black-box knowledge-asymmetry attack (ICLR 2026): compares RAG output against standalone-LLM output to isolate knowledge-base-derived text, refining queries iteratively with no prior corpus knowledge — [arXiv 2507.23229](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.23229v2)
- Backdoor-planted data extraction from RAG is a studied variant — [arXiv 2411.01705](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.01705)
- Proposed RAG defenses: input filtering that rejects extraction-intent queries, retrieval similarity thresholds, and mixing public documents into the private corpus so attack queries pull irrelevant material — [External Data Extraction Attacks against RAG](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.02964); a 2026 benchmark notes RAG attack/defense evaluation is not yet standardized — [arXiv 2602.09319](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.09319)
- Embedding inversion: an iterative correct-and-re-embed method (vec2text) recovers 92% of 32-token inputs *exactly* from state-of-the-art text embeddings, including full names from clinical notes — [Morris et al., Text Embeddings Reveal (Almost) As Much As Text, EMNLP 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.06816)

### Inferences
- The two architectures fail differently: a leaky adapter leaks to *anyone who can sample the model* and the leak is baked in until retrained; a RAG system leaks only what retrieval surfaces per query, the leak is revocable (delete the document), and per-document access control is enforceable at the retriever. For a GDPR right-to-erasure posture, RAG is categorically easier: erasure = delete row + embedding, whereas erasure from an adapter means retraining it.
- Residual RAG risks for this platform: (1) anyone with assistant query access can, with patience, walk a large fraction of the corpus out through the context window — mitigate with per-user retrieval scoping (only the author's own corpus is retrievable by the author) and recitation-length limits on output; (2) the vector store is corpus-equivalent under vec2text — encrypt and access-control embeddings exactly like the plaintext; (3) backups/logs that capture prompts will contain retrieved private text.
- In Chip Huyen's *AI Engineering* framing (ch. 7), the progression is prompt → few-shot examples (≤~50) → RAG → fine-tune, with the heuristic that fine-tuning buys *form* (voice, style) and RAG buys *facts* — [chapter](https://oreilly.com/library/view/ai-engineering/9781098166298/ch07.html); [reader's notes](https://alexstrick.com/posts/2025-01-26-notes-on-ai-engineering-chip-huyen-chapter-7-finetuning.html) (the "form vs facts" phrasing is from the notes; verify against the book before quoting as Huyen's). The privacy analysis aligns with the pedagogy: the thing a style-LoRA buys (form, voice) is precisely what memorization-prone generation tasks leak, so "RAG now, LoRA later only with an extraction eval" follows both the engineering-cost and the privacy ordering.

### Gaps
- No study found of RAG extraction attacks against a *single-user* system where the attacker is not a legitimate user (i.e., whether prompt-injection via ingested third-party content can trigger corpus exfiltration in a personal assistant) — the demonstrated attacks all assume query access.
- RAG defense efficacy numbers are inconsistent across papers; the 2026 benchmark explicitly says the field lacks a unified evaluation, so no defense can be cited with a reliable %-reduction.

## Adapter weights as sensitive artifacts: storage and handling practice

### Takeaway
Treat a LoRA adapter trained on private writing as a lossy compressed copy of the corpus, not as code: weights-only access provably enables membership inference, and in the nearest studied setting (diffusion LoRAs) enables reconstruction of the private training data. Published practice is thin — hosted providers isolate fine-tunes per account, and research proposes adapter encryption/watermarking — so handling policy mostly has to be derived: encrypt at rest, scope access like the corpus itself, and include the adapter in the erasure path.

### Cited Findings
- Weights-only reconstruction (nearest evidence, diffusion models): an adversary holding only shared LoRA fine-tuned weights can train an encoder that reconstructs the private fine-tuning images; DP-based defenses did not fully prevent this without hurting utility — [Risks When Sharing LoRA Fine-Tuned Diffusion Model Weights](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2409.08482)
- Adapters usable as membership/backdoor oracles without the training data — [LoRA as Oracle](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2601.11207); white-box adapter-weight inspection is an established threat/defense model (backdoor scanning) — [LoRAScan](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.06795), [weight-space backdoor detection](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2602.15195)
- Research artifacts for adapter protection exist: encryption of foundation models and LoRA adapters — [LoREnc](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.13163); white-box watermarks entangled into LoRA weights against removal/obfuscation — [SEAL](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2501.09284)
- Hosted-provider practice (OpenAI): fine-tuned models are private to the account that created them; fine-tuning data falls under API data policies (not used to train OpenAI models); one forum-era statement says uploaded fine-tuning files are retained until the customer deletes them — [OpenAI community](https://community.openai.com/t/will-my-fine-tuning-data-remain-private/32213), [walled-off thread](https://community.openai.com/t/are-fine-tuned-models-walled-off-or-is-it-safe-to-use-ip-for-training-fine-tuning-models/192824); OpenAI's privacy overview — [PDF](https://cdn.openai.com/global-affairs/openai-privacy-designed-with-intelligence.pdf). A 2026 third-party post claims training data is deleted after job completion, conflicting with the retention statement, and notes customers cannot verify deletion — [DEV Community](https://dev.to/tiamatenity/fine-tuned-models-remember-everything-the-training-data-privacy-problem-4a9e) (both are non-authoritative; current official docs not found in this search)
- A 2023 attack was demonstrated through a hosted fine-tuning API itself — [arXiv 2307.16382](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.16382?context=cs)

### Inferences
- Handling policy that follows from the evidence: store the adapter with the same classification as the corpus (for this platform: the sensitive/external-personal tier, encrypted at rest alongside the writing itself); never publish or share the adapter file (HuggingFace-style adapter sharing is the exact threat surface of the diffusion-weights paper); keep it out of ordinary artifact stores, CI caches, and backups that have broader read access than the corpus.
- GDPR consequence: the adapter is personal data derived from the corpus. Right-to-erasure of the author's writing must delete the adapter (and any checkpoints/optimizer states), not just the source documents and embeddings — retraining-or-deletion is the only compliant response, since no reliable "unlearning" of a specific document from an adapter is established practice.
- Checkpoints, eval logs (which contain canaries and extraction-probe outputs quoting the corpus), and tensorboard-style sample dumps are shadow copies with the same sensitivity as the adapter.

### Gaps
- No authoritative, current (2025–2026) official provider documentation page on fine-tuned-artifact storage/handling was retrievable in this search — only community forums, a policy PDF, and third-party blogs; retention specifics conflict between sources.
- No demonstrated end-to-end *text* reconstruction attack from LLM LoRA weights alone (the reconstruction result is from diffusion models); for text LLMs the proven weights-only capability is membership inference plus the standard query-based extraction once the adapter is served.
- No published industry standard (NIST/OWASP-level) specifically for adapter-weight handling was found.
