# US-14.5.1 — If You Never Confirm, Your Account Is Erased Within a Day

## 1. User Story

> **As a** person who signed up and then changed my mind — or never got the email — **I want** the account to erase itself rather than sit there holding my address **so that** abandoning a signup is the same as never making one.

**Retro-story (spec-of-record).** This behaviour shipped in Issues #124 (A2) / #179 / #373 and had no story file. Written 2026-08-06 from the code; every claim cites the implementation.

**The user-visible promise, stated plainly:** *Register, never click the confirmation link, and everything The Stacks holds about you — your email address, your password hash, your handle — is deleted, through the same right-to-erasure path a signed-in reader gets when they ask. The clock is the confirmation link's: once the link no longer works, the account behind it goes.* Worst case, if a stranger keeps requesting fresh links on your address, is eight days (see §4).

This is a **GDPR-positive** behaviour, not a cleanup job with a user-facing side effect. Storage limitation (Art. 5(1)(e)) says personal data must not be kept longer than necessary for the purpose, and the purpose of an unconfirmed signup expires when its link does.

**What the user does:**
1. Registers at `/register`. An account row and a signed confirmation link are created.
2. Does nothing.
3. Within 24 hours of the link's issue, the link stops verifying.
4. At the next 09:00 UTC, the account is erased — **or sooner**, if anyone registers in the meantime (§7's second path).
5. The address is free again.

**And the address is freed immediately for the person who needs it.** If the reader comes back and registers the *same* address, their earlier unconfirmed attempt is erased on the way in — **regardless of the TTL**, because *"if someone is registering this exact address, the earlier unconfirmed attempt is abandoned regardless of how recent it is, and holding the address hostage for 24 hours serves nobody"* (`accounts.ex:650-652`). So the "email has already been taken" wall for an account nobody owns does not happen. This is two erasure paths, not one, and §7 covers both.

**What the user sees:** nothing, which is the point. There is no "we deleted your account" email — sending one would mean retaining the address in order to tell someone we had stopped retaining it, and mailing an address whose owner never confirmed they wanted mail from us.

**Acceptance Criteria:**
- An unconfirmed account whose confirmation link no longer verifies is erased.
- Erasure goes through `GDPR.Deletion.delete_user_data/2` — the same path as a user-requested erasure — not a bare `Repo.delete`.
- A confirmed account is never touched.
- An account whose link is **still live** is never touched, however old it is.
- Resending a link cannot keep an account alive indefinitely.
- **Erasure does not depend on a cron alone.** Re-registering the same address frees it immediately, whatever the TTL says, and a registration also clears a small batch of other abandoned signups.
- A reap that fails never fails the registration that triggered it.
- Dead server-side auth state (expired tokens, dead refresh-token families) is swept on its own schedule.

## 2. UI Interaction Flow

There is no UI. This story is a promise the system keeps on the reader's behalf; its user-facing halves live in **US-14.1.1 (register)** and **US-14.4.2 (resend confirmation)**.

### The flow, as the reader experiences it
1. Reader registers → US-14.1.1. `email_confirmation_token` is signed with `Phoenix.Token.sign(endpoint, "email_confirm", user_id)`.
2. Reader either clicks the link (→ `Stacks.Email.confirm_email/1`, account confirmed, this story never applies) or does not.
3. Reader may ask for another link → US-14.4.2 → a fresh token, and 24 more hours.
4. If no link is ever clicked, the reaper erases the account.

### Sad Paths
- **Reader clicks a link at hour 30, after their 24-hour link died but before 09:00 UTC**: the link fails (`verify_confirmation_token` → `:error`) and they are told to request another. If they do so before the reaper runs, the account survives and gets a fresh 24 hours.
- **Reader clicks a link at hour 30 having requested a new one at hour 23**: the new link is live, so the reaper leaves the account alone. This is the whole reason for #373's redesign (§5).
- **Erasure fails partway** for one account: logged at error with the failing `Ecto.Multi` step, the other accounts in the batch still process, and the job returns `{:error, :partial_failure}` so Oban retries (up to 3 attempts).
- **The node is scaled to zero at 09:00 UTC**: the cron does not fire. Per ROOT H, Oban cron only runs while a node runs. **This is the case the registration-time path exists for** — it is not left as an accepted risk. See §7.
- **Reaping fails during someone else's registration**: swallowed and logged at warning. *"A registration must not fail because someone else's abandoned signup could not be erased"* (`accounts.ex:621-623`). The nightly job is the backstop.

## 3. API Calls

**No endpoint of its own**, and nothing a client can call to inspect, delay, or query this. But one existing endpoint **triggers** it as a side effect:

### `POST /api/auth/register`
- **Auth**: None · **Pipeline**: `:api`, `:rate_limit_auth`
- **Relevance**: `Accounts.register/1` calls `reap_abandoned_signups/1` **before** creating the new account (`accounts.ex:612`). So every registration erases up to five abandoned signups — the caller's own prior attempt first, then a batch of others.
- **Invisible from outside**: the response does not depend on whether anything was reaped, and failures are swallowed. So this is not an account-existence oracle either.

That last point is the constraint the whole surface is designed around: an endpoint reporting whether an unconfirmed account exists for an address would be exactly such an oracle. `Accounts.confirmation_resendable?/1` is careful about the same thing — *"A refusal is invisible from outside — the endpoint's response does not depend on it — so this cannot become an account-age oracle"* (`accounts.ex:454-462`) — and the reap inherits that property by being silent.

## 4. Auth & Middleware Guards

No HTTP surface, so no plugs. The guards that matter here are the **clocks**, and there are two ceilings:

### The TTL — 24 hours
`@unverified_account_ttl_seconds 24 * 60 * 60` (`accounts.ex:43`). This is simultaneously:
- how long a confirmation link verifies (`Phoenix.Token.verify(…, max_age: @unverified_account_ttl_seconds)`), and
- the reaper's prefilter cutoff.

**One constant, spoken once.** `verify_confirmation_token/1` is *"the ONE place the `email_confirm` salt and its `max_age` are spoken. Both readers of a confirmation link go through here — `Stacks.Email.confirm_email/1` when the reader clicks it, and `confirmation_link_live?/1` when the reaper asks whether that click would still work — so the two cannot drift into disagreeing about which links are alive"* (`accounts.ex:421-428`).

### The absolute ceiling — 7 days
`@unverified_account_max_lifetime_seconds 7 * 24 * 60 * 60` (`accounts.ex:59`). Past this age no further confirmation link is issued, so the last one expires within a TTL and the reaper takes the account. **Absolute worst case is 7 days + 1 TTL = 8 days**, no matter how many times resend is pressed.

⛔ **Why the ceiling exists, in the code's own words:** `POST /api/auth/resend-confirmation` is necessarily **unauthenticated** — the reader cannot sign in, that being the problem. So the caller is not provably the data subject, and without a ceiling *a stranger who posts someone else's address every 23 hours could prevent that person's abandoned signup from ever being erased.* Storage limitation cannot be left in the hands of an anonymous caller. The cap is enforced when the link is **issued** rather than when the account is reaped, so a link is never issued that would outlive the account behind it (`accounts.ex:51-58`).

## 5. Database Interactions

### Read: candidate accounts
- **Table**: `op.users`
- **Query**: `where u.email_confirmed == false and u.created_at < ^cutoff`, selecting `{id, email_confirmation_token}`
- **Function**: `Accounts.expired_unverified_ids/1`

### Decide: is the link still live?
Not a database operation at all — `Phoenix.Token.verify/4` on each candidate's token.

⛔ **The reaper's clock is the LINK's clock, not `created_at`.** This used to be `created_at < now - ttl` alone, which was correct only because registration was the sole issuer of a confirmation token — creation time and issue time were the same instant. `POST /api/auth/resend-confirmation` broke that: a resend mints a freshly signed token good for a further TTL from the moment it is signed. Against the old rule, *a reader who lost their email at hour 23, asked for another, and got a link good until hour 47 would have had the account underneath it erased at hour 24 — a live link pointing at nothing, which is strictly worse than never offering the button.* Two clocks that only agreed by accident are now one (`accounts.ex:376-394`).

So the SQL is a cheap **prefilter** (nothing younger than the TTL can hold a dead link) and the **decision** is the same `Phoenix.Token.verify/4` call the reader's click makes. Whatever the reaper erases, the link was already going to refuse; whatever the link still accepts, the reaper leaves alone. A missing token (`nil`) is a dead link and is reaped.

### Read: the abandoned account for a specific address (Path A only)
- **Table**: `op.users`
- **Query**: `where fragment("lower(?)", u.email) == ^normalised and u.email_confirmed == false` — **no age predicate**, deliberately (§1)
- **Function**: `Accounts.abandoned_id_for_email/1` (private)

### Write: erase
- **Function**: `Stacks.GDPR.Deletion.delete_user_data/2` with `reason: "unverified account expired — email never confirmed within TTL"` (both paths) and `actor: "expired-unverified-reaper"` (Path B) or `actor: "system:registration_reap"` (Path A)
- **Tables**: whatever the erasure path covers — `op.users` and every user-FK'd table the schema guard proves reachable, plus the `event_log` PII scrub
- **Transaction**: `Ecto.Multi` inside `delete_user_data/2`; a failure returns `{:error, step, reason, changes}` and the step name is logged
- **Audit**: `delete_user_data/2` writes its own audit row — so a reaped account leaves a record that it was reaped and why, which is what makes the erasure inspectable without retaining the data

⚠️ **Using the full erasure path is the design decision, not a convenience.** An unverified account has never logged in, so it carries no bookshelves, placements, comments or sessions — the erasure is cheap, and a bare `Repo.delete` on `op.users` would have worked *today*. It would stop working the moment registration or onboarding starts writing anything else, and it would silently skip the `event_log` scrub of the UUID-only `user.registered` event. One erasure path means new personal data is covered by construction (`expired_unverified_accounts_job.ex:8-13`).

### The companion sweep — dead auth state
`Stacks.Workers.GuardianTokenSweepJob` reaps two tables that otherwise grow without bound:

| Table | What is dead | How |
|-------|--------------|-----|
| `op.guardian_tokens` | An access token whose 8h TTL elapsed without an explicit logout leaves a row behind (`Guardian.revoke/1` only deletes on logout) | `Guardian.DB.Token.purge_expired_tokens/0` — one indexed `DELETE … WHERE exp < now()` |
| `op.auth_token_families` | A refresh-token family revoked more than 7 days ago, **or** whose `session_started_at` is past the absolute session cap plus the same 7-day grace | `Repo.delete_all` on that disjunction |

⛔ **Live families are never deleted.** A family that is unrevoked and within the cap matches neither predicate. The 7-day retention is conservative rather than load-bearing: a revoked family is already rejected by the `verify_claims` gate, so retention affects table size, not security (`guardian_token_sweep_job.ex:33-36`).

The session cap is read from the **same** config key as `StacksWeb.AuthController` and converted through the same `Stacks.Duration.to_seconds/1`, so *"the two never disagree on how long a session may live" is structural rather than a comment asking politely* (`guardian_token_sweep_job.ex:74-82`).

## 6. Event Flow & Lifecycle

### Events Emitted
Whatever `GDPR.Deletion.delete_user_data/2` emits for any erasure — this story adds none of its own.

⚠️ **A dedicated `user.reaped` event would be a mistake.** It would put a row in the immutable `event_log` about an account whose entire point was to leave nothing behind; erasure can only scrub payloads, not delete rows. The audit row `delete_user_data/2` already writes is the right home for the fact.

### Events Scrubbed
The UUID-only `user.registered` event emitted at registration is scrubbed by the same erasure path — identical to any other erasure.

### Telemetry
`[:stacks, :accounts, :unverified_reaped]` with measurements `%{erased: n, failed: n}` and no metadata (`expired_unverified_accounts_job.ex:49-53`). No user ids, no addresses — telemetry is warehouse-adjacent.

### Event Handlers Triggered
None directly.

## 7. Background Jobs (Oban)

### There are TWO erasure paths, and the second is not a job

⛔ **The important one for correctness is the synchronous one, and it exists because a cron cannot carry a retention obligation on this platform.** `Accounts.register/1`'s own comment states the reasoning in full (`accounts.ex:600-611`):

> *"`ExpiredUnverifiedAccountsJob` was the only thing erasing accounts that never confirmed their email, which made a cron entry the sole guarantee for a **retention** obligation — and nothing user-triggered could substitute, because the person who abandoned a signup by definition never comes back. With the platform scaling to zero, that job may not fire at all.*
>
> *Registration is the right moment for two reasons. It is the event most correlated with signup abandonment, so the work arrives roughly in proportion to the debt. And an abandoned account currently **blocks** a real one: someone re-registering the same address gets 'email has already been taken' for an account nobody owns."*

This is the same "work should arrive in proportion to the thing that creates the need" argument that `BookCreatedHandler` makes for enrichment (see US-2.6.1 §6) — applied to a *retention* duty rather than a feature, which is why it matters more here.

#### Path A — `Accounts.register/1` → `reap_abandoned_signups/1` (synchronous, **private**)
- **Not a job.** A `defp` in `Stacks.Accounts` (`accounts.ex:627`), called from `register/1` before the new account is created.
- **Selects**: `abandoned_id_for_email(email) ++ expired_unverified_ids()`, `Enum.uniq`, `Enum.take(@reap_batch)` where `@reap_batch = 5`
- **`abandoned_id_for_email/1` deliberately ignores the TTL** — it matches on `lower(email) == normalised and email_confirmed == false`, any age (§1)
- **Erases via** `Deletion.delete_user_data/2`, `actor: "system:registration_reap"` — the *same* erasure path, a different actor string, which is what makes the two paths distinguishable in the audit log
- **Bounded and cheap on purpose**: *"an unverified account has never logged in, so it carries no bookshelves, placements, comments or sessions"*
- **Failure handling**: whole function wrapped in `rescue` → `Logger.warning` → `:ok`. A registration must not fail over someone else's abandoned signup.
- ⚠️ **Not transactional across the batch.** `Enum.each` over per-id `delete_user_data/2` calls, each its own `Ecto.Multi`. So "in the same transaction" is only true *within* one account's erasure, and any cross-cutting requirement (releasing a consumed invitation, say) has to live **inside `GDPR.Deletion.delete_user_data/2`**, not in this function. See §16.

#### Path B — the nightly job, as a backstop
*"The nightly job stays as a backstop"* (`accounts.ex:625`). It is the only path that runs on a platform with no registrations, which is exactly the low-traffic case where an abandoned signup would otherwise sit indefinitely.

### `Stacks.Workers.ExpiredUnverifiedAccountsJob`
- **Queue**: `:default` · **Max attempts**: 3
- **Args**: none — `perform(_job)`
- **Schedule**: `{"0 9 * * *", …}` — daily at 09:00 UTC, chosen as an otherwise-empty slot (`config/config.exs`, crontab)
- **Uniqueness**: none configured. Harmless: two concurrent runs would race on the same ids and the second `delete_user_data/2` finds nothing.
- **What it does**:
  1. `Accounts.expired_unverified_ids()` — prefilter + per-candidate token verify
  2. For each id, `GDPR.Deletion.delete_user_data/2` with the reaper's reason and actor
  3. Tally `{erased, failed}`, logging each failure with its `Ecto.Multi` step
  4. `Logger.info` the tally, emit the telemetry event
- **On success**: `:ok`
- **On partial failure**: `{:error, :partial_failure}` → Oban retries the whole batch. Already-erased accounts drop out of the candidate query, so a retry is not double work.

### `Stacks.Workers.GuardianTokenSweepJob`
- **Queue**: `:default` · **Max attempts**: 3
- **Args**: none
- **Schedule**: `{"0 0 * * *", …}` — daily at midnight UTC
- **What it does**: purge expired `op.guardian_tokens`; delete provably-dead `op.auth_token_families`; log both counts
- **On failure**: retried by Oban. Nothing here is user-visible; a missed run means two tables are one day larger.

**Why the Guardian access-token TTL matters to this story:** `config :core, Stacks.Accounts.Guardian, ttl: {8, :hours}` exists *so that the reaper has expired rows to purge* — without a TTL a token never expires and the store grows unbounded (`config/config.exs`, Guardian block). The sweep and the TTL are one design, not two.

## 16. Known Gaps

1. **The cron only fires while a node runs** (`min_machines_running = 0`, ROOT H) — **but this is already mitigated, not an open gap.** The registration-time path (§7 Path A) exists precisely for it. What remains is narrower: on a platform with **no registrations at all**, only the cron can run, so an abandoned signup on a dormant deployment can outlive its stated lifetime. The absolute-ceiling SQL check in §13 is the instrument that would catch it; nothing alerts on it.
   *(Corrected 2026-08-06: an earlier draft of this section listed the cron as the sole path and the scale-to-zero case as an accepted risk. That was wrong — `Accounts.register/1:612` was missed. The lesson is the ordinary one: grepping `workers/` for a reaper finds jobs, not the synchronous path that mattered more.)*
2. **`op.admin_sessions` is not swept** by `GuardianTokenSweepJob` or anything else. Inert rows, but an unbounded table — see US-20.1.1 §7.
3. **The 24-hour figure is not surfaced to the reader anywhere.** The promise in §1 is kept by the system and stated in no UI copy, so a reader cannot know it. A line on the registration or resend surface would make the guarantee legible; it belongs in US-14.1.1 / US-14.4.2.
4. **No metric on the absolute ceiling.** §13's last-but-one row is the check that would prove the 8-day worst case holds, and it exists only as a SQL query written here — not as a dashboard panel or an alert.
5. **Release-on-erasure is specified in US-14.1.3, and it is opt-in — not a property of erasure.** An invitation consumed by a since-reaped signup must be **restored** rather than burnt, or a reader who mistypes their email is permanently locked out by a retention job. Two things about where that lives:

   - **It cannot live in `reap_abandoned_signups/1`.** That is a `defp` with no transaction spanning the batch, and **Path B never calls it** — so the requirement would silently not apply to cron-reaped accounts, which is most of them. The performing site is `GDPR.Deletion.delete_user_data/2`, the one seam all callers share.
   - ⛔ **But it must NOT fire on every erasure.** An earlier draft of this gap said "so both paths and a user-requested erasure all get it". That was wrong, and wrong in the dangerous direction: a real participant who redeemed an invitation and used the platform for months would, on erasing their own account, **make the code in that months-old invitation live again for anyone who still has it.** Consumption is not undone by the consumer leaving; only an *abandoned* signup — never confirmed, so never a participant — leaves the key genuinely unspent. US-14.1.3 specifies a **`restore_invite:` opt defaulting to `false`**, passed `true` by the two reap paths only; user-requested erasure and owner break-glass omit it. Corrected 2026-08-06 after `launch-stories` caught it.

   ⚠️ **And the restore cannot be a separate `Ecto.Multi` step from the GDPR scrub.** The scrub nulls `redeemed_by_id`, which is the only column identifying *which* invitation to restore — so a scrub that lands first makes the restore match nothing, silently, on every reap forever. US-14.1.3 settles both in one step with restore-then-scrub ordering. Both branches of the opt need testing: a test that only drives the reap path would let the credential resurrection ship.

   Do not re-derive this here — US-14.1.3 is the spec of record, and this entry exists so a reader of the reaper story does not reinvent it wrongly.
6. **Path A's `@reap_batch = 5` is unexplained.** The per-email reap is unbounded and correct; the extra batch of five others is a rate chosen without a stated consumer, unlike (say) `DiscoverEditionsJob`'s cap of ten which is argued against what the price layer consumes. Five registrations to clear five stale accounts is fine while abandonment is low and quietly insufficient if it is not — and nothing measures the backlog.
